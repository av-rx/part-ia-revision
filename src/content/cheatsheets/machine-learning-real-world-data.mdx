## 1 · Sentiment classification fundamentals

### The task and lexicon-based approach

**Sentiment classification** — decide whether a review is positive or negative from its text. Standard NLP task; IMDb reviews (with the author's **star rating** as ground truth) are the running example.

A **sentiment lexicon** lists ~8000 words, each with a **polarity** (positive/negative) and **intensity** (strong/weak). **Hypothesis:** a review with more positive than negative words is positive overall.

- **Strengths:** simple, no training data required.
- **Weaknesses:** built from human intuition — static, can't adapt to language change (e.g. "terrific", "sick" shifting meaning); fails on **negation** ("wasn't exciting") and **context-dependent sentiment**; some words ambiguous (e.g. "care"); took many hours of manual labour.

### Tokenisation

**Tokenisation** — splitting text into individual words (**tokens**) before lexicon lookup. Whitespace splitting alone is insufficient: sentence-initial capitalisation, attached punctuation, contractions, and hyphens all need handling.

- **Token** — any individual occurrence of a word in text.
- **Type** — a unique word form. "the cat sat on the mat" → 6 tokens, 5 types ("the" repeats).

### Evaluation: accuracy

$$A = \frac{c}{c+i}$$

where $c$ = correct decisions, $i$ = incorrect decisions.

### Improving the simple lexicon classifier

- **Threshold adjustment** — classify positive only if $\text{positive} \geq \text{negative} + \text{threshold}$ (requires a margin).
- **Lexicon strength weighting** — strong-intensity words contribute more than weak ones.
- **Negation handling** — detect negation words (`not, no, never, n't, none, nobody, nothing, neither, nowhere, hardly, barely`); flip the next word's polarity until end-of-line punctuation (`. ! ?`) resets the flag.
- **Word frequency weighting** — weight by $\log(1 + \text{count})$ where count is the word's frequency in the review.

These can be combined, but improvements are **not always additive** — in practice the best single classifier on IMDb data (frequency + strength weighting + threshold) reached ~71% from a 63.6% baseline, beating the "everything combined" variant (~67%). Further improvements: intensifiers, better token normalisation, multi-word sentiment expressions, ML.

## 2 · Naive Bayes classifier

### From lexicon to ML

**Machine learning** — a program that adapts its behaviour from data exposure without explicit programming. Instead of a fixed lexicon, learn which words signal sentiment from labelled data. **Features** — automatically observable properties (here: words in a review). **Classes** — labels not directly observable; $C = \{\text{POS}, \text{NEG}\}$. **Classification** — a function mapping features → classes.

### Probabilistic classifiers and Bayes' theorem

A probabilistic classifier returns $P(c_i \mid O)$ for observed features $O = \{w_1, \ldots, w_n\}$ and predicts $\hat c = \arg\max_{c \in C} P(c \mid O)$.

**Bayes' theorem:** $P(c \mid O) = \dfrac{P(c)\,P(O \mid c)}{P(O)}$. $P(O)$ is constant across classes for a given document, so drop it:

$$c_{\text{NB}} = \arg\max_{c \in C} P(c\mid O) = \arg\max_{c \in C} P(c)\,P(O \mid c)$$

**Worked example (brain tumour):** $P(T) = 0.00006$ (prior), $P(H\mid T) = 0.6$ (likelihood), $P(H) = 0.5$ (marginal). Posterior $P(T \mid H) = \frac{0.6 \times 0.00006}{0.5} = 0.000072$ — the strong prior dominates; a headache barely moves tumour probability.

### Naive independence assumption

All features (words) are assumed conditionally independent given the class: $P(O \mid c) = P(w_1, \ldots, w_n \mid c) \approx \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i \mid c)$. Full classifier:

$$c_{\text{NB}} = \arg\max_{c \in C} P(c)\prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i \mid c)$$

This independence assumption is **clearly false** (word order/context matters) but NB works well regardless.

### Training, testing, and parameter estimation (MLE)

**Training** — estimate $P(w_i\mid c)$ and $P(c)$ from labelled data. **Testing** — apply the learned model to unseen data. **Supervised** learning (labels used in training). **Never test on training data.**

**Prior:** $\hat P(c) = \dfrac{N_c}{N_{\text{rev}}}$. **Word likelihood:** $\hat P(w_i \mid c) = \dfrac{\text{count}(w_i, c)}{\sum_{w \in V}\text{count}(w,c)}$

### Log-space computation

Products of many small probabilities underflow — convert to sums:

$$c_{\text{NB}} = \arg\max_{c \in C}\left[\log P(c) + \sum_{i=1}^n \log P(w_i\mid c)\right]$$

### The zero-probability problem and smoothing

If $\text{count}(w_i,c)=0$, then $P(w_i\mid c)=0$ → the whole product (or, in log-space, the sum) collapses to $-\infty$. A single unseen word can zero out an entire class regardless of other evidence.

**Add-one (Laplace) smoothing:** $\hat P(w_i\mid c) = \dfrac{\text{count}(w_i,c)+1}{\left(\sum_{w\in V}\text{count}(w,c)\right) + |V|}$. $V$ must be the **full training vocabulary** (both classes). **Generalised smoothing** with tunable parameter $\omega$ (add-one is $\omega=1$):

$$\hat P(w_i\mid c) = \frac{\text{count}(w_i,c)+\omega}{\left(\sum_{w\in V}\text{count}(w,c)\right) + \omega|V|}$$

In practice, smoothing improved IMDb NB accuracy from ~49% (unsmoothed) to ~80% (add-one) — a massive jump, making smoothing essential, not optional.

## 3 · Statistical properties of language: Zipf's and Heaps' laws

### Zipf's Law

Why does smoothing help so much? The answer lies in the statistical distribution of words in natural language. Word frequency is inversely proportional to its frequency rank:

$$f_w \approx \frac{k}{r_w^{\alpha}}$$

($f_w$ = frequency, $r_w$ = rank, 1 = most frequent; $\alpha, k$ language-dependent). General form with rank-shift $\beta$: $f_w \approx \dfrac{k}{(r_w+\beta)^\alpha}$.

- $\alpha \approx 1$ for English, $\alpha \approx 1.3$ for German.
- Most frequent words are **function words** (the, of, and; der, die, und; que, de, no) — low semantic content, high frequency.
- **Log-space:** $\log f_w = \log k - \alpha \log r_w$ — linear. A log-log plot of frequency vs rank is approximately a straight line; a least-squares fit gives $\alpha = -m$ (negative gradient) and $k = e^c$ (intercept).
- Near-Zipfian distributions occur widely: city sizes, web page access frequency, earthquake magnitudes, word senses per word, musical note frequencies, machine instruction frequencies.
- Fitted to an IMDb corpus, predictions track actual frequencies reasonably for common neutral words but deviate more for sentiment-bearing words (e.g. "amazing", "heartrending"), which occur far more/less often than the global rank-frequency law predicts.

### Heaps' Law

Relates vocabulary size to text length:

$$u_n = k\,n^\beta$$

($u_n$ = number of types/vocabulary size, $n$ = number of tokens; $\beta \approx \tfrac{1}{2}$, $30 \le k \le 100$). **No saturation** — vocabulary never stops growing as text grows, though new types get rarer (**diminishing returns**), never reaching zero growth. **Log-space:** $\log u_n = \log k + \beta \log n$ — linear. Example: Moby Dick has 206,052 tokens but only 16,000 types; a large IMDb corpus shows the same near-linear log-log growth.

### Connecting the laws to Naive Bayes

Zipf's law → a **long tail** of many rare word types, each rare individually but collectively holding significant probability mass. Heaps' law → no matter how much training data, **new unseen types will always appear** in new text. MLE assigns **all** probability mass to seen types (since probabilities sum to 1) → **overestimates** seen-type probability and gives unseen types probability 0 → zero-probability problem. Smoothing **redistributes** probability mass from MLE's overestimate of seen types to unseen types — a better (though imperfect) estimate of the true distribution.

## 4 · Significance testing

### Motivation and null hypothesis

A higher observed accuracy doesn't prove System A is better — the difference might be chance (documents vary in style/length/vocabulary, favouring different systems). **Null hypothesis $H_0$** — the two systems perform equally well (results come from the same distribution). Choose significance level $\alpha$ (e.g. 0.05 or 0.01) **before** testing. **Reject $H_0$** with confidence $1-\alpha$ if the observed result is sufficiently unlikely under $H_0$.

### The sign test

A **non-parametric, paired** test using binary per-document outcomes:

- **Positive**: System 1 correct, System 2 wrong (A beats B on this document).
- **Negative**: System 2 correct, System 1 wrong.
- **Tie/null**: both agree (both correct or both wrong).

Under $H_0$, $q = 0.5$ (either system equally likely to win). Number of negative events out of $N$ follows $B(N,q)$:

$$P_q(X=k\mid N) = \binom{N}{k}q^k(1-q)^{N-k}$$

Cumulative (at most $k$ negatives):

$$P_q(X\le k\mid N) = \sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{N}{i}q^i(1-q)^{N-i}$$

If $P(X\le k)$ is small (below $\alpha$), reject $H_0$.

**One-tailed** — tests a specific direction (prior belief about which system wins). **Two-tailed** — tests either direction; more conservative. By symmetry of $B(N,0.5)$: if $2P(X\le k)\le \alpha$, the difference is significant. **Two-tailed is standard.**

### Treatment of ties

Ignoring ties (standard textbook advice for continuous distributions) causes **unjustified rejection of $H_0$** (Type I error) when ties are common, as in classification. **Correct treatment:** add 0.5 to both the positive and negative counts for each tie, then round up:

$$N = 2\left\lceil\frac{\text{null}}{2}\right\rceil + \text{plus} + \text{minus}, \qquad k = \left\lceil\frac{\text{null}}{2}\right\rceil + \min(\text{plus},\text{minus})$$

The final p-value (two-sided):

$$p = 2\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{N}{i}q^i(1-q)^{N-i}$$

### Type I and Type II errors

| | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
|---|---|---|
| **Reject $H_0$** | Type I error (false positive), probability $\alpha$ | Correct |
| **Fail to reject** | Correct, probability $1-\alpha$ (**specificity**) | Type II error, probability $\beta$ |

**Power** = $1-\beta$ — probability of correctly detecting a real difference. **Fix Type II (low power):** a more powerful test (e.g. permutation test), more data, or strengthen the effect. **Type I errors should never happen** if methodology is correct — usually a sign of the wrong test or misapplication.

Significance tests can only show a **difference** — cannot prove two distributions are the same. Reject $H_0$ → "significantly better." Fail to reject → **inconclusive** (not proof of equality — could be insufficient data or no real effect).

### Effect size, significant digits, and the three deadly sins

**Effect size** — the measured difference (continuous). **Significance** — binary flag. Report **both**; a claim of "better" without significance testing is meaningless — only a **delta** can be significant, never a single value. Report only as many decimal places as are **meaningful** (where adjacent values at that precision would plausibly be significant) — 6 decimal places when significance fails at the 1st/2nd decimal is a hallmark of inexperience (or fraud).

**The three deadly sins of significance reporting:**
1. **Methodological unsoundness** — no test performed, but "better"/"outperform" claimed from raw numbers.
2. **Scientific illiteracy** — test performed, but "better" claimed anyway for insignificant differences.
3. **Scientific fraud** — no test, "better" claimed, and "significant" used to describe large effect sizes with no statistical backing.

### Confusion matrix

| | Predicted POS | Predicted NEG |
|---|---|---|
| **Actual POS** | TP | FN |
| **Actual NEG** | FP | TN |

Useful for troubleshooting — shows whether errors skew toward one class. Generalises to $n$ classes.

## 5 · Overtraining and cross-validation

### Generalisation and overtraining

A good classifier **generalises** — performs well on new, unseen data, learning general (not training-specific) patterns. Never test on training data.

**Overtraining (overfitting)** — measured performance improves but real generalisation worsens; the classifier has learned accidental training-data properties. Also called Type III errors / "testing hypotheses suggested by the data."

- **The Wayne Rooney effect** — vocabulary associated with people/genres shifts over time; a classifier trained on 2004 reviews may degrade on 2015/16 reviews.
- **Overtraining via repeated test-set use** — repeatedly tuning against the same test set means the classifier indirectly learns its accidental properties; it's no longer truly "unseen."
- **Detecting overtraining:** safe with large amounts of test data and **new** test data each evaluation; risky with repeated incremental improvements against the same small test set. Diagnostic: inspect the most characteristic features per class — unlikely-to-generalise features signal overtraining.

### N-fold cross-validation

1. Split data randomly into $N$ equal folds.
2. For each fold $X$: train on the other $N-1$ folds, test on fold $X$.
3. Report the **average** of the $N$ fold performances.

Every datapoint is tested exactly once and trained on $N-1$ times; never both trained and tested at once.

**Variance across folds:**

$$\text{var} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2, \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i$$

Low variance across folds = stable result.

**Variants:**
- **Stratified CV** — each fold mirrors the overall class distribution.
- **Leave-one-out (jack-knifing)** — $N$ = number of datapoints.
- **Dependency-sensitive CV** — folds isolate known characteristics (e.g. one fold per genre/source).

**Significance under CV:** treat all $N$ test folds as **one combined experiment** — count positives/negatives/nulls across the full combined set, not $N$ separate tests. This gives more data and can reveal significance otherwise undetectable.

### Validation corpus

CV still uses data "seen" in some sense. For incremental feature engineering or hyperparameter tuning, use a separate **validation (development) corpus** — never used for training or testing. Uses: setting hyperparameters (e.g. smoothing $\omega$) before train/test; **stopping criterion** for feature engineering (stop when validation performance starts to drop). The **test corpus** is evaluated only once, after all development is complete.

**Data splits in practice:** training set (fit model) / validation set (tune + stop) / test set (final, once). For CV: pool training+validation, fold over the pool; keep test separate.

### Confusion matrix (general form)

| | System POS | System NEG | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Truth POS** | $V$ | $W$ | 900 |
| **Truth NEG** | $X$ | $Y$ | 900 |
| **Total** | $V{+}X$ | $W{+}Y$ | 1800 |

Diagonal ($V$, $Y$) = correct; off-diagonal ($W$, $X$) = errors — generalises to $n$-class problems.

## 6 · Inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss' kappa)

### Motivation

Binary POS/NEG is an oversimplification — many reviews are **neutral**: **lukewarm** (mildly evaluative, "it was ok") or **pro-con** (lists both good and bad — balanced, not indifferent). Star-rating-derived ground truth can mislead here. Original author intent is unrecoverable post-publication. **Claim:** human agreement is the only empirically available "truth" for subjective judgements — the more independent humans agree, the "truer" a label is, motivating measurement of inter-annotator agreement.

### Observed agreement $\bar P_a$

With $k$ raters, average ratio of observed to possible pairwise rater-rater agreements:

$$\bar P_a = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} S_i, \qquad S_i = \frac{\sum_j \binom{n_{i,j}}{2}}{\binom{k}{2}}$$

where $n_{i,j}$ = number of raters choosing label $j$ for item $i$, $\binom{k}{2} = \frac{k(k-1)}{2}$ = possible pairs.

### Chance agreement $\bar P_e$

High $\bar P_a$ alone is insufficient — could just reflect a dominant class. For categories $j=1\ldots n$ with observed proportion $p_j$:

$$\bar P_e = \sum_{j=1}^{n} p_j^2$$

$\bar P_e$ increases with skew (one class dominates), decreases with balance or more categories. E.g. two classes $(0.5,0.5) \to \bar P_e = 0.5$; two classes $(0.95,0.05) \to \bar P_e \approx 0.905$; four balanced classes $\to \bar P_e = 0.25$.

### Fleiss' kappa

$$\kappa = \frac{\bar P_a - \bar P_e}{1 - \bar P_e}$$

Numerator = agreement above chance; denominator = maximum possible above-chance agreement. From the annotation table ($N$ items, $k$ raters, $n$ categories, $C_j = \sum_i n_{i,j}$):

$$\bar P_a = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N S_i, \qquad \bar P_e = \sum_{j=1}^n \left(\frac{C_j}{Nk}\right)^2$$

| $\kappa$ | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 1 | Perfect agreement |
| 0 | No agreement beyond chance |
| < 0 | Worse than chance |
| ≥ 0.8 | Very good (Krippendorff 1980) |

No universally accepted full scale — only the extremes have standard meaning. $\kappa$ is affected by the number of categories (more categories → lower $\bar P_e$ → can inflate $\kappa$) and can mislead with small samples.

**Worked example:** $N=29$ items, $k=4$ raters, $n=5$ categories, $p_j$ = 0.362, 0.026, 0.319, 0.069, 0.224 → $\bar P_e = \sum p_j^2 = 0.288$. With $\bar P_a = 0.5804$: $\kappa = \frac{0.5804-0.288}{1-0.288} \approx 0.41$ (moderate agreement).

### Practical findings (NB + significance + kappa)

- A three-way NB (POS/NEG/NEUTRAL) typically scores much lower under 10-fold CV than binary NB (e.g. ~0.59 vs ~0.82) — the extra neutral class is hard.
- Random vs stratified folds give similar mean accuracy and low variance — CV is generally stable. Held-out vs later (e.g. 2016) samples usually differ only within CV variance, so no strong evidence of drift, though small held-out samples make any single comparison unreliable.
- The simple lexicon classifier degrades much more than NB when moved to a new time period — illustrates the Wayne Rooney effect concretely.
- Sign-test p-values stabilise once enough paired examples are available (~200); a marginal classifier improvement (e.g. magnitude weighting alone) may *not* be significant against a baseline, while NB vs a weaker classifier often *is* ($p<0.05$).
- Kappa varies a lot by annotator cohort and class balance: cohorts with near-50/50 splits and a neutral category give low-moderate $\kappa$ (~0.3–0.4), while clear-cut sentiment cohorts give high $\kappa$ (>0.8) — kappa reflects task difficulty as much as annotator quality.

## 7 · Hidden Markov Models

### Markov chains

A **Markov chain** is a stochastic process over finite, fully-observable discrete states where the next state depends only on the current one (**Markov / Limited Horizon Assumption**):

$$P(w_t\mid w_{t-1},\ldots,w_1) \approx P(w_t\mid w_{t-1}) \quad\Rightarrow\quad P(w_1,\ldots,w_T) \approx \prod_{t=1}^{T}P(w_t\mid w_{t-1})$$

Viewable as a probabilistic finite-state automaton. **Transition matrix** $A$: $a_{ij}=P(w_t{=}j\mid w_{t-1}{=}i)$, rows sum to 1. Applications: speech recognition (phone sequences), dialogue systems (speech-act sequences), predictive texting. Limitation: states must be **unambiguously observable**.

### HMMs: hidden states + observations

In many real tasks, underlying states aren't directly observable — only noisy **observations** are. An **HMM** adds an observation layer on top of a Markov chain: hidden states $X_1,\ldots,X_T$ evolve as a Markov chain, and each observation $O_t$ depends only on $X_t$.

**First-order HMM assumptions:**
1. **Markov assumption**: $P(X_t\mid X_1\ldots X_{t-1}) \approx P(X_t\mid X_{t-1})$
2. **Output independence assumption**: $P(O_t\mid X_1\ldots X_T,O_1\ldots O_T) \approx P(O_t\mid X_t)$

### Formal definition

- $S_e=\{s_1,\ldots,s_N\}$ — emitting hidden states; $s_0$ start, $s_f$ end (no real observations). $K=\{k_1,\ldots,k_M\}$ — output alphabet; $k_0,k_f$ start/end symbols.
- $O = O_1\ldots O_T$ — observation sequence; $X=X_1\ldots X_T$ — hidden state sequence.
- **Transition** $a_{ij}=P(X_t{=}s_j\mid X_{t-1}{=}s_i)$; $A$ is $(N{+}2)\times(N{+}2)$, $\forall i:\sum_{j=0}^{N+1}a_{ij}=1$. Transitions into $s_0$ and out of $s_f$ are undefined.
- **Emission** $b_i(k_j)=P(O_t{=}k_j\mid X_t{=}s_i)$; $B$ is $(M{+}2)\times(N{+}2)$.
- HMM fully defined by $\mu=(A,B)$.

| Task | Observations | Hidden states |
|---|---|---|
| Speech recognition | Audio signal | Phonemes |
| POS tagging | Words | POS tags |
| Machine translation | Target words | Source words |

**Running example (loaded-die HMM):** a fraudulent croupier alternates a fair die ($P(O)=\tfrac16$ each face) and a loaded die. States: loaded $s_1$, fair $s_2$, plus $s_0,s_f$. $a_{11},a_{22}$ are self-loops; $a_{12},a_{21}$ are switches; emission distributions differ (fair ≈ uniform, loaded biased toward one face).

### The four fundamental HMM problems

| # | Problem | Given | Find | Algorithm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Labelled learning | parallel $O$, $X$ | $A,B$ | relative frequency (+ smoothing) |
| 2 | Unlabelled learning | $O$, state set $S_e$ | $A,B$ | Baum-Welch (EM) |
| 3 | Likelihood | $\mu=(A,B)$, $O$ | $P(O\mid\mu)$ | Forward, $O(N^2T)$ |
| 4 | Decoding | $\mu=(A,B)$, $O$ | best $X$ | Viterbi, $O(N^2T)$ |

### Parameter estimation (Problem 1)

$$a_{ij} = P(X_{t+1}{=}s_j\mid X_t{=}s_i) \approx \frac{\text{count}_{\text{trans}}(s_i\to s_j)}{\text{count}_{\text{trans}}(s_i\to\cdot)}$$

$$b_i(k_j) = P(O_t{=}k_j\mid X_t{=}s_i) \approx \frac{\text{count}_{\text{emit}}(s_i,k_j)}{\text{count}_{\text{emit}}(s_i,\cdot)}$$

Add-one smoothing applies for unseen transitions/emissions.

### Decoding: Viterbi algorithm

**Goal:** $\hat X = \arg\max_X P(X,O\mid\mu) = \arg\max_{X_{1\ldots T}}\prod_{t=1}^T P(O_t\mid X_t,B)\,P(X_t\mid X_{t-1},A)$. Brute force is $O(N^T)$, infeasible. **DP applies** because of **optimal substructure** (the optimal full sequence contains the optimal prefix sequence for every $j$) and **overlapping subsolutions** (probabilities for earlier $t$ are reused), reducing $O(N^T)\to O(N^2T)$. **Trellis**: $(N{+}2)\times(T{+}2)$ DP table, rows = states, columns = time.

- **Viterbi probability** $\delta_j(t)$ — probability of the best path ending in $s_j$ at time $t$: $\delta_j(t) = \max_{1\le i\le N}\left[\delta_i(t-1)\,a_{ij}\,b_j(O_t)\right]$
- **Backpointer**: $\psi_j(t) = \underset{1\le i\le N}{\arg\max}\left[\delta_i(t-1)\,a_{ij}\,b_j(O_t)\right]$

**Algorithm steps:**

1. **Initialisation** ($t=0$): $\delta_j(0)=b_j(O_0)$, $\psi_j(0)=s_0$.
2. **Main step** ($t=1\ldots T$): for each $s_j$, $\delta_j(t)=\max_i[\delta_i(t-1)a_{ij}b_j(O_t)]$, $\psi_j(t)=\arg\max_i[\cdots]$.
3. **Termination**: connect to end state via $a_{j,f}$: $\delta_f(T{+}1)=\max_j[\delta_j(T)\,a_{j,f}\,b_f(k_f)]$, $\psi_f(T{+}1)=\arg\max_j[\cdots]$.
4. **Backtrace**: follow $\psi$ from $\psi_f(T{+}1)$ backwards, then reverse to get $\hat X_1,\ldots,\hat X_T$.

**Why keep all $N$ states per timestep:** a greedy "keep only the best state" approach fails — a state that looks suboptimal at $t$ may become optimal at $t'>t$ once a later observation is seen. Since the future is unknown, all $N$ (but no more) per-step probabilities must be kept. **Numerical stability:** run in log-space (sums replace products), as with NB.

### Evaluation: precision, recall, F-measure

Used when evaluating detection of one class of interest (e.g. the "loaded die"):

| Metric | Formula | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Precision $P$ | $\frac{TP}{TP+FP}$ | of predicted positives, fraction correct |
| Recall $R$ | $\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$ | of true positives, fraction detected |
| F-measure $F$ | $\frac{2PR}{P+R}$ | harmonic mean |
| Accuracy | $\frac{TP+TN}{TP+FP+FN+TN}$ | overall fraction correct |

Preferred over accuracy when classes are **unbalanced** (accuracy dominated by majority class).

### Practical findings (loaded-die HMM)

Transition/emission matrices estimated by relative frequency typically show very high self-transition probabilities for both "fair" and "loaded" states (long runs before switching, expected run length $\approx \frac{1}{1-a_{ii}}$) and rare switches between them; "fair" emits roughly uniformly over faces while "loaded" is strongly skewed toward one face. Viterbi decoding on such data typically achieves precision/recall/F1 all around 0.85, consistent between a single train/test split and 10-fold CV — the model reliably identifies the loaded-die state, with results stable rather than split-dependent.

## 8 · K-means clustering

### Clustering, distance, and normalisation

**Clustering** — unsupervised grouping of datapoints by feature similarity; no labels or graph structure given. Output: cluster assignment $z_n \in \{1,\ldots,K\}$ per datapoint $x_n$. Unlike classification, no gold labels during training.

Most common distance: **Euclidean distance (L2 norm)** for $D$-dimensional $a,b \in \mathbb{R}^D$:

$$\text{distance}(a,b) = \sqrt{\sum_{d=1}^D (a_d-b_d)^2} = \|a-b\|$$

Features on very different scales distort Euclidean distance — **normalisation is essential**:

- **Min-max scaling** → $[0,1]$: $x'_d = \dfrac{x_d-\min(X_d)}{\max(X_d)-\min(X_d)}$.
- **Standardisation (Z-score)** → zero mean, unit variance.

### K-means algorithm and WCSS objective

**Input:** $X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_N\}$, number of clusters $K$. **Output:** assignments $z_n$. (1) **Initialise** — pick $\mu_k$ for $k=1\ldots K$ at random. (2) **Repeat until convergence:** **Assignment** $z_n \leftarrow \arg\min_k \|\mu_k - x_n\|$; **Update** $\mu_k \leftarrow \text{MEAN}(\{x_n : z_n=k\})$.

**Objective (WCSS, Within-Cluster Sum of Squares):** $L(X,Z) = \sum_{n=1}^N \text{distance}(x_n,\mu_{z_n})^2$

### Convergence and K-means++ initialisation

Two equivalent stopping criteria: assignments unchanged, or means unchanged. K-means is **guaranteed to converge** (WCSS non-increasing every step) but **not** guaranteed to find the global minimum — depends on initialisation. Best practice: multiple random initialisations, keep the lowest WCSS.

**K-means++:** (1) draw $\mu_1$ uniformly at random from $X$; (2) for $k=2:K$, compute each point's squared distance to its nearest existing mean $D_{k-1}(x) = \min_{k'=1}^{k-1}\|x-\mu_{k'}\|^2$, define probability $p(\mu_k=x_n) = \frac{D_{k-1}(x_n)}{\sum_{n'} D_{k-1}(x_{n'})}$, and sample $\mu_k \sim p$. Points far from existing means are more likely chosen → better spread, reducing (but not eliminating) initialisation sensitivity.

### Limitations and practical findings

- $K$ must be specified in advance.
- Assumes **spherical, equal-variance** clusters — poor on elongated/ring-shaped/non-convex structure.
- Sensitive to initialisation (K-means++ helps, doesn't fix). Running K-means multiple times per $K$ typically shows: very small $K$ (e.g. $K=2$) gives identical WCSS across seeds (almost any initialisation finds the same partition), while moderate $K$ shows a noticeable spread between runs (multiple reachable local minima) — confirming convergence ≠ global optimum. WCSS decreases monotonically as $K$ increases, which is expected but does not imply more meaningful clustering.

## 9 · Clustering evaluation

### Intrinsic vs extrinsic

**Intrinsic** — quality from data + cluster structure only, no external labels. **Extrinsic** — compare against gold-standard class labels. Cluster IDs carry no semantic meaning and different runs may renumber clusters — harder than classification evaluation.

### Intrinsic: WCSS

$$L(X,Z) = \sum_n \text{distance}(x_n,\mu_{z_n})^2$$

Rewards cohesion but is **trivially minimised** by one cluster per point (WCSS=0) and **monotonically decreasing in $K$** — cannot select $K$ alone.

### Intrinsic: Silhouette score

For datapoint $x_n$:

- **Cohesion** (centroid-based): $\text{coh}(x_n) = \text{distance}(x_n,\mu_{z_n})$
- **Separation**: $\text{sep}(x_n) = \min_{k\neq z_n}\text{distance}(x_n,\mu_k)$
- **Silhouette**: $\text{silhouette}(x_n) = \dfrac{\text{sep}(x_n)-\text{coh}(x_n)}{\max(\text{coh}(x_n),\text{sep}(x_n))}$

Average over all points; range $[-1,1]$.

| Score | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| > 0.7 | Good — well-separated, cohesive |
| 0.5–0.7 | Reasonable |
| 0.25–0.5 | Weak structure |
| ≈ 0 | Overlapping clusters |
| < 0 | Wrong assignments |

Unlike WCSS, silhouette has a meaningful peak vs $K$ — usable to **choose $K$** (maximise average silhouette).

**Datapoint-based variant** (used when no explicit centroids, or required by spec):

$$\text{coh}(x_n) = \frac{1}{|K_{z_n}|-1}\sum_{x'\in K_{z_n}}\text{distance}(x_n,x'), \qquad \text{sep}(x_n) = \min_{k\neq z_n}\frac{1}{|K_k|}\sum_{x'\in K_k}\text{distance}(x_n,x')$$

Note: silhouette is **not invariant to squaring** — must use true Euclidean distance (with `sqrt`), unlike K-means assignment.

### Extrinsic: Rand Index and Adjusted Rand Index

Pairwise accuracy: for every pair of points, were they correctly placed together or apart? Given datapoints $X$, clustering $\mathcal{K}=\{K_1,\ldots,K_k\}$, gold classes $\mathcal{C}=\{C_1,\ldots,C_c\}$:

$$\mathcal{T} = \{(x_i,x_j) : x_i,x_j \in K_m \text{ and } x_i,x_j \in C_n\} \quad\text{(correctly together)}$$
$$\mathcal{S} = \{(x_i,x_j) : x_i\in K_{m_1}, x_j\in K_{m_2}, x_i\in C_{n_1}, x_j\in C_{n_2}\} \quad\text{(correctly separated)}$$

$$\text{RandIndex} = \frac{|\mathcal{T}|+|\mathcal{S}|}{\binom{d}{2}}$$

**Problem:** most pairs are correctly separated by chance (TN dominates) → RI values crowd near 1 regardless of clustering quality. **Adjusted Rand Index** corrects for chance (analogous to Cohen's Kappa):

$$\text{AdjustedRI} = \frac{RI - \text{expected\_RI}}{1-\text{expected\_RI}}$$

Near 0 = chance level; near 1 = near-perfect agreement.

### Mutual information / NMI

Model cluster assignments $Z$ and class labels $Y$ as distributions $P(z_n{=}k)$, $P(y_n{=}c)$. **Mutual information:** $\mathcal{I}(Z;Y) = H(Z)-H(Z\mid Y) = H(Y)-H(Y\mid Z)$. **Normalised Mutual Information:**

$$\text{NMI}(Z,Y) = \frac{2\,\mathcal{I}(Z;Y)}{H(Z)+H(Y)}$$

Symmetric, $[0,1]$ — 0 = no correlation, 1 = perfect. Also needs chance-adjustment — larger $K$ artificially inflates MI.

### Soft clustering and take-home messages

**Hard clustering** — each point assigned to exactly one cluster (K-means). **Soft clustering** — graded membership, a probability distribution over clusters per point (applications: document tagging, image labelling, word-sense disambiguation, social network analysis).

Clustering is powerful for **exploratory analysis** but evaluation is inherently hard; a "good" clustering is application-dependent — define the downstream task first. Avoid evaluating against labels the model never saw (defeats the purpose of unsupervised discovery); if labels exist and matter, prefer a **supervised** model.

### Practical findings (songs dataset, genre as extrinsic label)

Sweeping $K$ over a wide range on an audio-features dataset against genre labels: silhouette typically peaks at a *small* $K$ (e.g. $K=3$, "weak" 0.25–0.5 range) — clusters exist but aren't sharp; WCSS keeps decreasing (useless for picking $K$ alone); Rand Index and NMI both tend to keep *increasing* with $K$, favouring much larger $K$ than the true genre count. This **disagreement is informative**: intrinsic (silhouette) and extrinsic (RI/NMI) measures can favour very different $K$, because RI is inflated by TN dominance and more clusters can always fit gold labels more finely. NMI staying well below 1 even at large $K$ shows genre labels are only moderately aligned with the audio feature space — genre is partly a human construct not fully captured by the features.

## 10 · Social networks: properties and small-world phenomenon

### Network types and basic graph properties

Networks model entities + relationships as graphs: Facebook (people/"friend"), Twitter (entities/"follows", "retweets"), citation networks (papers/"cites"). Analysis types: **node role** (gatekeeper, connector) and **substructure** (dense regions/cliques). Applications: sociology/economics, identifying influencers and subcommunities, information-flow modelling (fake news, ads), epidemic modelling, sentiment + network analysis.

This course models networks as **undirected, unweighted** graphs.

- **Distance** $d(u,v)$ — shortest path length between $u,v$.
- **Diameter** — $\max_{u,v} d(u,v)$, the longest shortest path.
- **Degree** $\deg(v)$ — number of neighbours. Degree distributions can be highly skewed (e.g. Erdős had 509 co-authors).

### Small-world phenomenon

Many natural networks exhibit **sparse connectivity** (relatively few edges, not complete), **closely clustered regions** (dense local neighbourhoods), **short average path length** (grows only logarithmically with network size), and **high clustering coefficient** (neighbours of a node tend to be neighbours of each other).

**Six degrees of separation:** Karinthy (1929) conjectured ≤5 intermediaries connect any two people; Milgram (1967) partially verified via letter-passing. Non-obvious: such short paths exist **and** are findable by humans using only local information (**decentralised search**). Watts & Strogatz showed random graphs with high local triadic closure plus a few long-range links reproduce the small-world property.

### Key structural concepts

- **Giant component** — connected component containing most nodes.
- **Weak ties** — socially distant, infrequent links (acquaintances); often hold giant components together. **Strong ties** — close friends/family.
- **Bridge** — edge connecting two otherwise-disconnected components. **Local bridge** — edge $A$–$B$ sharing no common neighbours; cutting it increases $d(A,B)$.
- **Triadic closure** — if $A$–$B$ and $A$–$C$, then $B$–$C$ likely forms.
- **Global clustering coefficient** — ratio of closed triads to all triads (open + closed). **Local clustering coefficient** of $A$ — probability two randomly chosen neighbours of $A$ are themselves neighbours.

### Computing network diameter

Use **BFS from every node** to get all-pairs shortest paths (APSP); diameter = max distance found. Complexity $O(V(V+E))$ for unweighted undirected graphs — valid because there are no weights/negative edges. Implementation: build an adjacency dictionary from an edge list (each edge adds both directions, since undirected); for each node, BFS gives shortest distances to all reachable nodes; diameter = max over all sources of the max distance.

## 11 · Betweenness centrality and the Brandes algorithm

### Definition

**Betweenness centrality** of $v$ — proportion of all-pairs shortest paths passing through $v$:

$$C_B(v) = \sum_{s,t\in V} \frac{\sigma(s,t\mid v)}{\sigma(s,t)}$$

where $\sigma(s,t)$ = total shortest paths $s\to t$ (1 if $s=t$), $\sigma(s,t\mid v)$ = those passing through $v$ (0 if $v\in\{s,t\}$).

High-betweenness nodes/edges are **gatekeepers** linking dense clusters — removing them isolates clusters (basis of hierarchical edge-removal clustering).

### Brandes algorithm — DP formulation

Naive computation is space-expensive; Brandes (2001/2008) uses DP to aggregate dependencies, giving total complexity $O(V(V+E))$. Applies because of **optimal substructure** (problem decomposes recursively into independent subproblems combining to the full solution) and **overlapping subproblems** (same subproblem revisited; caching saves work) — name both when justifying any DP algorithm in this course.

**Recursive shortest-path count:**

$$\sigma(s,t) = \sum_{u\in\text{Pred}(t)}\sigma(s,u), \qquad \text{Pred}(t)=\{u:(u,t)\in E,\ d(s,t)=d(s,u)+1\}$$

**Pairwise dependency:** $\delta(s,t\mid v) = \dfrac{\sigma(s,t\mid v)}{\sigma(s,t)}$. **One-sided dependency:** $\delta(s\mid v) = \sum_t \delta(s,t\mid v)$. Brandes' recurrence:

$$\delta(s\mid v) = \sum_{\substack{(v,w)\in E\\ d(s,w)=d(s,v)+1}} \frac{\sigma(s,v)}{\sigma(s,w)}\bigl(1+\delta(s\mid w)\bigr)$$

Final betweenness:

$$C_B(v) = \sum_{s\in V}\delta(s\mid v)$$

### Two-phase algorithm per source $s$

**Phase 1 — BFS forward pass (path counting):** initialise $\text{Pred}[w]=\varnothing$, $\text{dist}[t]=\infty$, $\sigma[t]=0$ $\forall t$; $\text{dist}[s]=0$, $\sigma[s]=1$; enqueue $s$. While queue non-empty: dequeue $v$, push onto stack $S$; for each neighbour $w$ of $v$ — **path discovery:** if $\text{dist}[w]=\infty$, set $\text{dist}[w]\leftarrow\text{dist}[v]+1$, enqueue $w$; **path counting:** if $\text{dist}[w]=\text{dist}[v]+1$, set $\sigma[w]\leftarrow\sigma[w]+\sigma[v]$ and append $v$ to $\text{Pred}[w]$.

**Phase 2 — back-propagation (dependency accumulation):** initialise $\delta[v]\leftarrow 0$ $\forall v$. While $S$ non-empty: pop $w$; for each $v\in\text{Pred}[w]$, $\delta[v]\leftarrow\delta[v]+\frac{\sigma[v]}{\sigma[w]}(1+\delta[w])$; if $w\neq s$, $C_B[w]\leftarrow C_B[w]+\delta[w]$.

### Undirected graphs

The algorithm above is for **directed** graphs. For undirected graphs each pair $(s,t)$ is counted twice (once per direction) — **halve all betweenness scores** at the end. Implementation: run the BFS forward pass + back-propagation for every source node, accumulating into a single `betweenness[v]` array initialised to 0, then divide every entry by 2.

## 12 · Network models and community detection

### Generative models

| Model | Mechanism | Property |
|---|---|---|
| **Erdős–Rényi $G(n,p)$** | each pair connected independently with probability $p$ | Poisson degree distribution |
| **Barabási–Albert** | preferential attachment (new nodes link to high-degree nodes) | scale-free, power-law degree |
| **Watts–Strogatz** | start regular lattice, randomly rewire a few edges | high clustering + low diameter (small-world) |

### Power-law degree distributions

$$P(k) \propto k^{-\gamma}$$

— **scale-free** networks; a small number of hub nodes have very high degree.

### Community detection: Girvan–Newman

Iteratively remove the **highest-betweenness edge**; track **modularity** $Q$ as the graph splits into components. $Q$ measures the fraction of intra-community edges minus the expected fraction at random — used to decide where to stop (peak $Q$ = best split into communities).

## Common pitfalls

- **Naive Bayes independence assumption is false** but the classifier works well anyway — state this explicitly ("naive" assumption, conditional independence given class).
- **Without smoothing, a single unseen word zeroes an entire class** — unsmoothed NB accuracy (~49%) vs smoothed (~80%) is the canonical illustration; always quote both Zipf's and Heaps' laws as the *why*.
- **Vocabulary $V$ for smoothing must include words from both classes** (full training vocabulary), not just the class being scored.
- **Ignoring ties in the sign test causes spurious significance (Type I errors)** — always use the 0.5-split tie correction and round up.
- **A failed significance test is not proof of equality** — report "inconclusive," never "no difference."
- **Effect size and significance are separate axes** — never say "better" without both; only deltas can be significant.
- **Cross-validation under significance testing: combine all folds into one test** — don't run $N$ separate tests.
- **WCSS always decreases with $K$** — never use it alone to pick $K$; use the elbow or silhouette instead.
- **K-means converges to a local, not global, minimum** — always run multiple initialisations (or K-means++) and report the spread/variance.
- **Rand Index is inflated by TN dominance** — prefer Adjusted RI or NMI for extrinsic clustering evaluation; RI's near-1 values aren't necessarily meaningful.
- **Viterbi needs log-space** for numerical stability over long sequences (sums replace products, as in NB).
- **Betweenness centrality must be halved for undirected graphs** — each shortest path between $s,t$ is counted from both directions.
- **Silhouette is not invariant to squaring distances** — unlike K-means assignment (which can use squared distance for efficiency), silhouette requires true Euclidean distance.

## Exam strategy

- For **sentiment classification**: state the independence assumption, write the smoothed probability formula explicitly with $\omega$ or $+1$, and connect zero-probability → Zipf/Heaps → smoothing in one coherent narrative.
- For **significance testing**: define $H_0$, state $\alpha$ before computing, write the two-sided binomial sum formula, and explicitly handle ties with the $\lceil \text{null}/2\rceil$ correction.
- For **cross-validation**: state the $N$-fold procedure, the variance formula, and (if asked about significance) emphasise pooling folds into one test.
- For **HMMs**: write $\mu=(A,B)$ explicitly, state both first-order assumptions, and give the Viterbi recurrence with both $\delta$ and $\psi$ — note the $O(N^2T)$ complexity and why all $N$ states must be kept per step.
- For **k-means**: state the WCSS objective formula, note convergence is guaranteed but only to a local minimum, and mention K-means++ when discussing initialisation.
- For **clustering evaluation**: distinguish intrinsic (silhouette, WCSS) from extrinsic (RI, ARI, NMI); note WCSS's monotonicity and RI's chance-inflation as the two standard "gotchas."
- For **social networks**: identify whether the graph is directed/weighted before applying any formula (this course defaults to undirected/unweighted); for betweenness, remember the halving step for undirected graphs.
- For **Brandes/DP algorithms**: name the two DP prerequisites (optimal substructure, overlapping subproblems) — examiners reward this framing for both Viterbi and Brandes.
