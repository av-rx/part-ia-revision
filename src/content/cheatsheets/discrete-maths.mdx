## 1 · Logic and propositions

**Proposition:** a statement that is either true or false.

**Connectives:**

| Symbol | Name | Truth |
|---|---|---|
| $\neg P$ | Negation | Opposite of $P$ |
| $P \wedge Q$ | Conjunction | True iff both true |
| $P \vee Q$ | Disjunction | True iff at least one true |
| $P \Rightarrow Q$ | Implication | False only when $P$ true, $Q$ false |
| $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ | Biconditional (iff) | $P$ true iff $Q$ true |

**Key equivalences:**
$$P \Rightarrow Q \equiv \neg P \vee Q \equiv \neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P \quad (\text{contrapositive})$$
$$\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \wedge \neg Q$$

**Converse** of $P \Rightarrow Q$ is $Q \Rightarrow P$ — **not** logically equivalent.
**Contrapositive** of $P \Rightarrow Q$ is $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ — always equivalent.

### Quantifiers

$$\forall x \in A.\, P(x) \qquad \exists x \in A.\, P(x) \qquad \exists! x \in A.\, P(x) \text{ (unique)}$$

**Negation rules:**
$$\neg(\forall x.\, P(x)) \equiv \exists x.\, \neg P(x)$$
$$\neg(\exists x.\, P(x)) \equiv \forall x.\, \neg P(x)$$

Swapping $\forall$ and $\exists$ changes meaning: $\forall x\,\exists y.\,R(x,y) \not\equiv \exists y\,\forall x.\,R(x,y)$.

## 2 · Proof techniques

### Direct proof

Assume $P$; derive $Q$ using definitions, lemmas, algebra. Label each step.

**Template:** "We prove $P \Rightarrow Q$ directly. Assume $P$. [Steps]. Therefore $Q$."

### Proof by contrapositive

Prove $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ instead of $P \Rightarrow Q$ (logically equivalent). Useful when $\neg Q$ gives more to work with than $P$.

**Template:** "We prove the contrapositive: assume $\neg Q$. [Steps]. Therefore $\neg P$."

### Proof by contradiction

Assume $P \wedge \neg Q$ (i.e. $P$ holds but $Q$ fails). Derive a contradiction $\bot$. Conclude $Q$ must hold whenever $P$ holds.

**Template:** "Suppose for contradiction that $P$ holds but $Q$ fails. [Steps reaching $\bot$]. Contradiction. Therefore $Q$ follows from $P$."

### Proof by construction

Prove existence by explicitly constructing the object. Most convincing form of existence proof.

### Disproof by counterexample

To refute $\forall x.\, P(x)$: exhibit one $x$ with $\neg P(x)$.

## 3 · Mathematical induction

### Ordinary induction

**Principle:** Let $P(m)$ be a statement for $m \in \mathbb{N}$. If $P(0)$ holds and $\forall n.\,(P(n) \Rightarrow P(n+1))$, then $\forall m.\,P(m)$.

**Template:**
1. State "We prove by the Principle of Induction."
2. Define $P(m)$ explicitly.
3. **Base case:** prove $P(0)$.
4. **Inductive step:** let $n \in \mathbb{N}$; assume $P(n)$ **(IH)**; prove $P(n+1)$.
5. Conclude "By induction, $P(m)$ holds for all $m \in \mathbb{N}$."

**Induction from basis $\ell$:** same but base case is $P(\ell)$ and step is $\forall n \geq \ell.\,(P(n) \Rightarrow P(n+1))$.

### Strong induction

**Principle:** Let $P(m)$ for $m \geq \ell$. If for all $n \geq \ell$, assuming $P(k)$ for all $k \in [\ell..n]$ implies $P(n+1)$, then $\forall m \geq \ell.\,P(m)$.

**Key difference:** the IH may assume $P$ at **all** prior values, not just $n$.

**When to use:** when $P(n+1)$ depends on more than one predecessor (e.g. Fibonacci, prime factorisation).

**Template:**
1. "We prove by the Principle of Strong Induction from basis $\ell$."
2. **Base case:** prove $P(\ell)$.
3. **Inductive step:** let $n \geq \ell$; assume $P(k)$ for all $\ell \leq k \leq n$ **(SIH)**; prove $P(n+1)$.
4. Conclude.

### Well-founded induction

Generalises ordinary and strong induction to any **well-founded relation** $\prec$ (one with no infinite descending chains). To prove $\forall a \in A.\,P(a)$: for each $x$, assuming $P(y)$ for all $y \prec x$ implies $P(x)$.

## 4 · Numbers and number theory

### Divisibility

$a \mid b$ (reads "$a$ divides $b$"): $\exists k \in \mathbb{Z}.\, b = k \cdot a$.

**Congruence:** $a \equiv b \pmod{m}$ iff $m \mid (a - b)$ iff $a$ and $b$ have the same remainder when divided by $m$.

**Congruence is an equivalence relation** on $\mathbb{Z}$; compatible with $+$ and $\times$.

$\mathbb{Z}_m = \{[0]_m, [1]_m, \ldots, [m-1]_m\}$ — the ring of integers modulo $m$.

**Division theorem (Euclidean division):** $\forall a \in \mathbb{Z}, b \in \mathbb{N}^+.\, \exists! q, r \in \mathbb{Z}.\, a = bq + r \wedge 0 \leq r < b$.

### GCD

$\gcd(a, b)$ = largest positive integer dividing both $a$ and $b$.

**Euclid's algorithm:**
$$\gcd(a, 0) = a \qquad \gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$$

Runs in $O(\log \min(a,b))$ steps. At each step: $\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$.

**Example:** $\gcd(252, 105) = \gcd(105, 42) = \gcd(42, 21) = \gcd(21, 0) = 21$.

**Bézout's theorem:** $\gcd(a, b) = ax + by$ for some $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$ (the **Extended Euclidean Algorithm** finds $x, y$).

**Theorem 92:** $\gcd(m, n)$ is the **least positive linear combination** of $m$ and $n$.

### Multiplicative inverses in $\mathbb{Z}_m$

**Corollary 93:** $[n]_m$ has a multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{Z}_m$ **iff** $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.

To find $[n]_m^{-1}$: run Extended Euclid's on $(m, n)$ to get $mx + ny = 1$; then $[n]_m^{-1} = [y]_m$.

**Coprime:** $\gcd(a, b) = 1$. When $m$ is prime, every non-zero element of $\mathbb{Z}_m$ has an inverse → $\mathbb{Z}_m$ is a field.

### Fermat's Little Theorem

**For prime $p$ and any $i \in \mathbb{N}$:** $i^p \equiv i \pmod{p}$.

**Equivalent form (for $\gcd(a, p) = 1$):** $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$.

**Proof by induction on $i$:**
- Base: $0^p = 0 \equiv 0$.
- Step: $(i+1)^p = i^p + p(\text{integer sum}) + 1 \equiv i + 1 \pmod{p}$ (by Binomial Theorem; middle terms divisible by $p$).

### Binomial theorem

$$(x + y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} y^k$$

**Pascal's rule:** $\binom{m+1}{k} = \binom{m}{k} + \binom{m}{k-1}$.

**Proof:** induction on $n$; inductive step uses Pascal's rule to split $\binom{n+1}{k}$.

### Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

**Every integer $\geq 2$ has a unique prime factorisation** (product of primes in non-decreasing order).

**Proposition 96 (existence):** proved by strong induction from basis 2. Base: $2$ is prime. Step: if $n+1$ is composite, it factors as $p \times q$ with $p, q \in [2..n]$, and each factor is prime or a product of primes by the SIH.

## 5 · Cryptography

### Diffie-Hellman key exchange

**Public parameters:** prime $p$, base $c$.

| Party | Secret | Computes | Sends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alice | $a$ | $\alpha = [c^a]_p$ | $\alpha$ |
| Bob | $b$ | $\beta = [c^b]_p$ | $\beta$ |

**Shared secret:** Alice computes $[\beta^a]_p = [c^{ab}]_p$; Bob computes $[\alpha^b]_p = [c^{ab}]_p$.

**Security:** eavesdropper sees $c, p, \alpha, \beta$. Recovering $a$ requires solving the **discrete logarithm problem**, which is computationally infeasible for large $p$.

### RSA encryption

**Setup:** choose prime $p$, encryption exponent $e$ with $\gcd(p-1, e) = 1$. Compute $d = [\text{lc}_2(p-1, e)]_{p-1}$ (so $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{p-1}$).

**Encrypt:** $k \mapsto [k^e]_p$ **Decrypt:** $\ell \mapsto [\ell^d]_p$

**Correctness (Lemma 94):** $e \cdot d = 1 + c(p-1)$ for some $c$, so $(k^e)^d = k^{ed} = k \cdot k^{c(p-1)} \equiv k \pmod{p}$ by Fermat's Little Theorem.

**Practical RSA (Lemma 95):** use modulus $N = pq$ (product of two large primes). Choose $d, e$ with $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{(p-1)(q-1)}$. Then $(k^e)^d \equiv k \pmod{N}$. Publish $N, e$; keep $p, q, d$ secret. Security relies on hardness of factoring $N$.

## 6 · Sets

### Basics

An **abstract set** is an unordered collection with no repetition. **Extensionality:** $A = B \iff \forall x.\, (x \in A \iff x \in B)$.

**ZFC axioms** (named, not memorised individually): extensionality, separation, powerset, pairing, union, infinity, choice, replacement, foundation.

**Russell's paradox:** $R = \{x \mid x \notin x\}$ is contradictory. Separation axiom prevents this by requiring $\{x \in A \mid P(x)\}$ — need a pre-existing set $A$.

### Set operations

$$A \cup B = \{x \mid x \in A \vee x \in B\}$$
$$A \cap B = \{x \mid x \in A \wedge x \in B\}$$
$$A \setminus B = A \cap B^c = \{x \in A \mid x \notin B\}$$
$$A^c = \{x \in U \mid x \notin A\} \quad \text{(complement w.r.t. universe } U\text{)}$$

**Powerset:** $\mathcal{P}(A) = \{X \mid X \subseteq A\}$. For finite $A$: $|\mathcal{P}(A)| = 2^{|A|}$.

### Boolean algebra of sets

$(\mathcal{P}(U), \emptyset, U, \cup, \cap, (\cdot)^c)$ is a Boolean algebra:

| Law | Form |
|---|---|
| Commutativity | $A \cup B = B \cup A$ |
| Associativity | $(A \cup B) \cup C = A \cup (B \cup C)$ |
| Distributivity | $A \cup (B \cap C) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C)$ |
| Idempotency | $A \cup A = A$ |
| Neutral | $A \cup \emptyset = A$; $A \cap U = A$ |
| Annihilator | $A \cup U = U$; $A \cap \emptyset = \emptyset$ |
| Absorption | $A \cup (A \cap B) = A$ |
| Complementation | $A \cup A^c = U$; $A \cap A^c = \emptyset$ |
| **De Morgan** | $(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c$; $(A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c$ |
| Double complement | $A^{cc} = A$ |

**Sets–logic correspondence:**
$\emptyset \leftrightarrow \text{false}$, $U \leftrightarrow \text{true}$, $\cup \leftrightarrow \vee$, $\cap \leftrightarrow \wedge$, $(\cdot)^c \leftrightarrow \neg$, $\subseteq \leftrightarrow \Rightarrow$, $= \leftrightarrow \Leftrightarrow$.

## 7 · Relations

### Binary relations

A **binary relation** $R : A \mapsto B$ is a subset $R \subseteq A \times B$. Write $a\,R\,b$ for $(a, b) \in R$.

**Composition:** $S \circ R = \{(a,c) \mid \exists b.\, a\,R\,b \wedge b\,S\,c\} : A \mapsto C$. Associative.

**Identity relation:** $\text{id}_A = \{(a,a) \mid a \in A\}$. Neutral element for composition.

### $n$-fold composition and paths

$R^{\circ 0} = \text{id}_A$; $R^{\circ(n+1)} = R \circ R^{\circ n}$.

$s\,R^{\circ n}\,t$ iff there is a **path of length $n$** from $s$ to $t$ in the directed graph $(A, R)$.

### Reflexive-transitive closure

$$R^{\circ *} = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} R^{\circ n}$$

$s\,R^{\circ *}\,t$ iff there is a path (of any length) from $s$ to $t$.

**Theorem:** $R^{\circ *}$ is the **least preorder** containing $R$ (least reflexive-transitive relation containing $R$).

### Boolean matrix representation

Relation $R : [m] \mapsto [n]$ ↔ $(m \times n)$ Boolean matrix. **Composition = Boolean matrix multiplication.** Reachability in finite graphs can be computed by iterating the adjacency matrix over $(\{\text{F},\text{T}\}, \vee, \wedge)$.

### Preorders and partial orders

**Preorder:** reflexive + transitive. **Partial order (poset):** preorder + antisymmetric.

Examples: $(\mathbb{R}, \leq)$, $(\mathcal{P}(A), \subseteq)$, $(\mathbb{N}, \mid)$ (divisibility).

## 8 · Functions

### Partial and total functions

- **Partial function** $f : A \rightharpoonup B$: each $a \in A$ maps to at most one $b$.
- **Total function (map)** $f : A \to B$: each $a \in A$ maps to exactly one $b$.

$$|A \to B| = |B|^{|A|} \qquad |A \rightharpoonup B| = (|B|+1)^{|A|}$$

**Composition** $(g \circ f)(a) = g(f(a))$ is associative; $\text{id}_A$ is its neutral element.

### Injections, surjections, bijections

| Property | Formal statement | Also called |
|---|---|---|
| **Injective** | $f(a_1) = f(a_2) \Rightarrow a_1 = a_2$ | One-to-one |
| **Surjective** | $\forall b.\, \exists a.\, f(a) = b$ | Onto |
| **Bijective** | Injective and surjective | One-to-one correspondence |

$f : A \to B$ bijective iff there exists a unique inverse $f^{-1} : B \to A$ with $f^{-1} \circ f = \text{id}_A$ and $f \circ f^{-1} = \text{id}_B$.

$A \cong B$ (isomorphic) iff a bijection between them exists. Same cardinality.

$|A| \leq |B|$ iff an injection $A \rightarrowtail B$ exists. $|A| \geq |B|$ iff a surjection $A \twoheadrightarrow B$ exists.

**Cantor-Bernstein-Schroeder:** $(A \lesssim B \wedge B \lesssim A) \Rightarrow A \cong B$.

### Finite cardinality

| Construction | Cardinality |
|---|---|
| $\mathcal{P}(A)$ | $2^{\#A}$ |
| $A \times B$ | $\#A \cdot \#B$ |
| $A \uplus B$ | $\#A + \#B$ |
| $A \to B$ | $\#B^{\#A}$ |
| $A \rightharpoonup B$ | $(\#B+1)^{\#A}$ |
| $\text{Bij}(A,A)$ | $(\#A)!$ |

### Equivalence relations and partitions

**Equivalence relation** $E$ on $A$: reflexive, symmetric, transitive.

**Equivalence class:** $[a]_E = \{x \in A \mid x\,E\,a\}$.

**Partition** of $A$: family of non-empty, pairwise disjoint blocks covering $A$.

**Theorem:** equivalence relations on $A$ correspond bijectively to partitions of $A$. The bijection sends $E \mapsto A/E$ (quotient set); inverse sends partition $P$ to the relation "same block."

**Bell numbers** $B_n = |\text{Part}([n])|$: $B_0=1, B_1=1, B_2=2, B_3=5, B_4=15$. Recurrence: $B_{n+1} = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \binom{n}{i} B_i$.

## 9 · Countability and Cantor's theorem

### Countability

- $A$ is **countable** if $A = \emptyset$ or there exists a surjection $\mathbb{N} \twoheadrightarrow A$ (an enumeration).
- $\mathbb{N}, \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}$ are countable; $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ is countable (Cantor pairing/anti-diagonals).

**Enumeration of $\mathbb{Z}$:** $0, -1, 1, -2, 2, -3, 3, \ldots$ (interleave non-negatives and negatives).

**Enumeration of $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$:** go along anti-diagonals: $(0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (2,0), (1,1), (0,2), \ldots$

### Cantor's diagonalisation

**Theorem 181:** For every set $A$, there is **no surjection** $A \twoheadrightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)$, so $|A| < |\mathcal{P}(A)|$.

**Proof:** Suppose $e : A \twoheadrightarrow \mathcal{P}(A)$. Let $D = \{x \in A \mid x \notin e(x)\}$. Since $e$ surjects, $\exists a$ with $e(a) = D$. Then $a \in D \iff a \notin D$ — contradiction.

**Corollary:** $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}) \cong (\mathbb{N} \to \{0,1\}) \cong \mathbb{R}$ is **uncountable**.

**Non-computability corollary:** the set of programs is countable; the set of functions $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ is uncountable — so most functions are non-computable.

### Lawvere's fixed-point theorem

**Theorem:** If $\exists$ surjection $e : A \twoheadrightarrow (A \to X)$, then every $f : X \to X$ has a fixed point.

**Proof:** let $h(x) = f(e(x)(x))$. By surjectivity, $\exists a$ with $e(a) = h$. Then $e(a)(a) = h(a) = f(e(a)(a))$ — so $e(a)(a)$ is a fixed point.

**Connection to Cantor:** take $X = \{0,1\}$; negation $x \mapsto 1-x$ has no fixed point → no surjection $A \twoheadrightarrow (A \to \{0,1\}) \cong \mathcal{P}(A)$.

## 10 · Formal languages

### Alphabet, words, languages

- **Alphabet** $\Sigma$: finite non-empty set of symbols.
- **Word (string):** finite sequence of symbols from $\Sigma$. $\epsilon$ = empty word.
- **Length** $|w|$: number of symbols. $|\epsilon| = 0$.
- **Concatenation:** $uv$ = $u$ followed by $v$. Associative; $\epsilon$ is neutral.
- **Language** $L$: any set of words, $L \subseteq \Sigma^*$ where $\Sigma^* = \bigcup_{n \geq 0} \Sigma^n$ is the set of all finite words over $\Sigma$.

### Regular expressions

Defined inductively:
- $\varnothing$ — empty language (no words).
- $\varepsilon$ — language $\{\epsilon\}$ (just empty word).
- $a$ (for $a \in \Sigma$) — language $\{a\}$.
- $RS$ — concatenation of $R$ and $S$.
- $R + S$ (or $R \mid S$) — union of $R$ and $S$.
- $R^*$ — Kleene star: $\{\epsilon\} \cup R \cup RR \cup \ldots$ (zero or more repetitions).

**Precedence:** $^* > \text{concat} > +$. So $ab^*c + d = ((a(b^*)c) + d)$.

### Deterministic Finite Automata (DFAs)

$$M = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F)$$

- $Q$: finite set of states.
- $\Sigma$: alphabet.
- $\delta : Q \times \Sigma \to Q$: transition function.
- $q_0 \in Q$: initial state.
- $F \subseteq Q$: accepting (final) states.

**Extended transition function** $\hat{\delta}$: $\hat{\delta}(q, \epsilon) = q$; $\hat{\delta}(q, wa) = \delta(\hat{\delta}(q, w), a)$.

**Accepts** $w$ iff $\hat{\delta}(q_0, w) \in F$.

**DFA representation:** state-transition table or state diagram (directed graph; double circles = accepting states).

### Non-deterministic Finite Automata (NFAs)

$$M = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F) \quad \text{where } \delta : Q \times (\Sigma \cup \{\varepsilon\}) \to \mathcal{P}(Q)$$

Multiple transitions possible from one state on the same symbol. $\varepsilon$-transitions allowed (move without consuming input). Accepts $w$ if **any** computation path reaches an accepting state.

**NFA ≡ DFA** in expressive power.

**Subset (powerset) construction:** convert NFA with $n$ states to DFA with $2^n$ states. DFA states = subsets of NFA states. Initial state = $\varepsilon$-closure$(q_0)$.

### Kleene's theorem

**Every regular language can be recognised by a DFA**, and vice versa. Equivalences:

$$\text{Regular expressions} \iff \text{NFAs} \iff \text{DFAs}$$

**Constructive proofs:**
- Regex → NFA: Thompson's construction (small NFA per operator).
- NFA → DFA: subset construction.
- DFA → Regex: state elimination.

### DFA minimisation

Two states $p, q$ are **distinguishable** if some word $w$ leads to acceptance from exactly one of them.

**Table-filling (Hopcroft-Moore) algorithm:**
1. Mark all pairs $(p, q)$ where one is accepting and the other is not.
2. Mark $(p, q)$ if $\exists a \in \Sigma$ such that $(\delta(p,a), \delta(q,a))$ is already marked.
3. Repeat step 2 until no new markings. Unmarked pairs = equivalent states.
4. Merge equivalent states; remove unreachable states.

### The pumping lemma for regular languages

**Lemma:** If $L$ is regular, then $\exists p \geq 1$ (pumping length) such that:
$$\forall w \in L \text{ with } |w| \geq p: \exists x, y, z \text{ with } w = xyz,\ |xy| \leq p,\ |y| \geq 1,\ \forall i \geq 0.\ xy^iz \in L.$$

**Usage (to prove $L$ is not regular):**
1. Assume $L$ is regular with pumping length $p$.
2. Choose a specific word $w \in L$ with $|w| \geq p$ (your choice!).
3. For **any** split $w = xyz$ with $|xy| \leq p$, $|y| \geq 1$: find $i$ such that $xy^iz \notin L$.
4. Contradiction → $L$ is not regular.

**Classic example:** $L = \{a^n b^n \mid n \geq 0\}$. Take $w = a^p b^p$. Any $y$ with $|xy| \leq p$ lies entirely in the $a$-prefix, so $y = a^k$ for some $k \geq 1$. Then $xy^0z = a^{p-k}b^p \notin L$ since $p-k \neq p$.

### Inductive rule definitions

Languages can be defined inductively via **inference rules**:
$$\frac{\text{premises}}{\text{conclusion}} \quad \text{(rule name)}$$

**Rule induction:** to prove $\forall x \in L.\, P(x)$ for an inductively-defined $L$: for each rule, assume $P$ holds for all premises; prove $P$ holds for the conclusion.

## Common pitfalls

- **Implication $\Rightarrow$ is false only when $P$ true and $Q$ false.** $P$ false → implication vacuously true (a common surprise).
- **Converse ≠ contrapositive.** Proving $Q \Rightarrow P$ does NOT prove $P \Rightarrow Q$.
- **Induction step must be universal.** Never assume the specific value you're proving; assume $P(n)$ for an arbitrary $n$, then derive $P(n+1)$.
- **Strong induction SIH:** "assume $P(k)$ for all $k \in [\ell..n]$" — explicitly state the range.
- **GCD is the LEAST positive linear combination** — Theorem 92. Do not confuse with Bézout (which says a linear combination exists; Theorem 92 says it's the smallest positive one).
- **Multiplicative inverse exists iff coprime to modulus.** In $\mathbb{Z}_6$: $[2]_6$ has no inverse (since $\gcd(6,2) = 2 \neq 1$).
- **Cantor's theorem:** the contradiction requires the diagonal set $D = \{x \mid x \notin e(x)\}$. Spell this out explicitly in proofs.
- **Lawvere is strictly more general than Cantor** — Cantor is the special case $X = \{0,1\}$.
- **NFA subset construction** can produce exponentially more states than the NFA. This is the price for determinism.
- **Pumping lemma: you choose $w$; adversary chooses the split.** Your proof must handle ALL valid splits, not just one.
- **$\varepsilon \in R^*$ always** — Kleene star includes zero repetitions.
- **$\{a^n b^n\}$ is not regular but is context-free** — don't confuse the hierarchy.

## Exam strategy

- **Induction proofs:** always write the 5-step template explicitly. Losing marks for "assume it holds at $n$" without labelling it (IH) is common.
- **Strong induction:** state the SIH as a range: "assume $P(k)$ for all $k \in [0..n]$" — not just "assume $P(n)$."
- **GCD calculations:** run Euclid's algorithm step by step. Extended Euclid's: work backwards substituting remainders.
- **RSA correctness:** the key equation is $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{p-1}$; Fermat's Little Theorem closes the proof.
- **Pumping lemma:** choose $w$ strategically so that $y$ (constrained by $|xy| \leq p$) must cause problems. State "for any valid split $xyz$..." and then show the contradiction.
- **DFA minimisation:** draw the table carefully; mark initial pairs (accepting vs non-accepting) first, then propagate.
- **Set proofs:** to prove $A = B$, prove $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$ separately using element arguments.
- **Cantor diagonalisation:** the proof is the same structure every time — know it cold.
