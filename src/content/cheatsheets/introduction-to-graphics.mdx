## 1 · Images, sampling, and pixel formats

### What is a digital image?

Two views: **computing** (2D array of pixels, discrete) vs **mathematical** ($I(x,y)$, a continuous intensity function). An image = **sampling** this function on a grid (choosing *where* to measure) + **quantisation** (choosing *how finely* to represent each measurement). A pixel is mathematically a dimensionless **point sample**, not a little square.

### Memory layout, stride, and indexing

Index formulas for pixel $(x,y)$, channel $c$, in a flat buffer:

- **Row-major (grayscale):** $i(x,y) = x + y \cdot n_{\text{cols}}$
- **Column-major:** $i(x,y) = x \cdot n_{\text{rows}} + y$
- **Interleaved row-major (colour):** $i(x,y,c) = x \cdot 3 + y \cdot 3 \cdot n_{\text{cols}} + c$
- **General (with strides):** $i(x,y,c) = x \cdot s_x + y \cdot s_y + c \cdot s_c$

`stride` = elements to advance per row — often **padded** to align rows to convenient boundaries (e.g. 4 bytes). **Padded images / ROI:** extra border pixels let operators safely read outside logical bounds, or address a sub-region directly:

$$i(x,y,c) = i_{\text{first}} + x \cdot s_x + y \cdot s_y + c \cdot s_c$$

For padding $p$, row-major interleaved colour: $i_{\text{first}} = (x_0+p)\cdot 3 + (y_0+p)(n_{\text{cols}}+2p)\cdot 3$, with $s_x = 3(n_{\text{cols}}+2p)$, $s_y = (n_{\text{cols}}+2p)\cdot 3$, $s_c = 1$.

### Pixel formats / bit depth

| Format | Bits/pixel | Colours |
|---|---|---|
| Grayscale | 8 (1 byte) | 256 levels |
| Highcolour | 16 (2 bytes) | $2^{16} = 65{,}536$ |
| Truecolour | 24 (3 bytes) | $2^{24} \approx 16.8$ million |
| Deepcolour | ≥32 (≥4 bytes) | even more |

**Colour banding:** visible stepping when too few bits represent a gradient — worsened by **Mach band / Chevreul illusion** (eye exaggerates edge contrast between flat regions). **Dithering** (controlled noise before quantisation) breaks up bands.

## 2 · 2D and 3D transformations (homogeneous coordinates)

### Basic 2D transforms and matrix form

- **Scale** by $m$: $x'=mx,\ y'=my$ — **Rotate** by $\theta$: $x'=x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,\ y'=x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta$ — **Translate** by $(x_0,y_0)$: $x'=x+x_0,\ y'=y+y_0$ — **Shear** (x-axis) by $a$: $x'=x+ay,\ y'=y$

$$\text{Scale: } \begin{bmatrix}m & 0\\0 & m\end{bmatrix} \quad \text{Rotate: } \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{bmatrix} \quad \text{Shear: } \begin{bmatrix}1 & a\\0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$$

A plain 2×2 matrix **cannot represent translation** (no constant term) — motivates homogeneous coordinates.

### Homogeneous coordinates

$(x,y) \to (x,y,w)$ with $(x,y,w) \equiv (x/w,y/w)$; conventionally $(x,y)\equiv(x,y,1)$. $w=0$ represents a **point at infinity** (a direction). 3×3 matrices represent scale/rotate/shear (leaving $w$ unchanged) **and now translation**:

$$\begin{bmatrix}x'\\y'\\w'\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&x_0\\0&1&y_0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\w\end{bmatrix} \implies x'=x+wx_0,\ y'=y+wy_0,\ w'=w$$

Dividing through: $\frac{x'}{w'} = \frac{x}{w}+x_0,\ \frac{y'}{w'}=\frac{y}{w}+y_0$.

### Concatenation, non-commutativity, scaling about a point

Combine transforms by **multiplying matrices**, applied **right-to-left** to a column vector: $v'=T_2T_1v$ means $T_1$ happens first. **Transforms do not commute** — translate-then-rotate ≠ rotate-then-translate.

**Scaling about an arbitrary point** $(x_0,y_0)$ by $m$ — the standard "sandwich" pattern used throughout graphics: translate $(x_0,y_0)\to$ origin, scale by $m$, translate back:
$$M = T(x_0,y_0)\, S(m)\, T(-x_0,-y_0)$$
Same pattern generalises to rotation about an arbitrary point/axis.

### 3D transformations

$(x,y,z,w)\to(x/w,y/w,z/w)$; matrices generalise to 4×4 (translation, scale, $R_x,R_y,R_z$). 3D transforms also **do not commute**. **Modelling example:** build a cylinder by composing translate/rotate/scale — rotational symmetry about its own axis means no third rotation step is needed. **Instancing:** define geometry once, place many copies via different model matrices.

## 3 · Projection, viewing, and the scene graph

### Types of projection

- **Parallel (orthographic):** e.g. $(x,y,z)\to(x,y)$ — discards depth, rays parallel; geometrically "correct" (CAD/engineering) but visually unrealistic.
- **Perspective:** e.g. $(x,y,z)\to(x/z,y/z)$ — objects shrink with distance $z$; how cameras/eyes work, looks realistic.

### Perspective projection: geometry and matrix form

Pinhole-camera setup: eye at origin looking along $+z$, image plane at $z=d$, $y$ up, $x$ right. By similar triangles:
$$x' = \frac{d\cdot x}{z}, \qquad y' = \frac{d\cdot y}{z}$$
(for $d=1$: $x'=x/z,\ y'=y/z$). Non-linear in $(x,y,z)$ — but becomes linear in homogeneous coordinates if division is deferred. A 4×4 matrix
$$P = \begin{bmatrix}d & 0 & 0 & 0\\0 & d & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$$
maps $(x,y,z,1)\to(dx,dy,z,z)$; dividing by $w'=z$ gives $(dx/z, dy/z, 1)$ — the **perspective divide**. Real matrices (e.g. OpenGL) additionally remap $z$ into $[-1,1]$ or $[0,1]$ using **near** ($n$) and **far** ($f$) planes for depth-buffer precision. **Key point:** clip space (post-matrix, homogeneous) → NDC requires $\div w$ — a classic mistake to forget.

### Arbitrary cameras and the MVP pipeline

Simple formulas assume a canonical camera (origin, looking down $z$, $y$ up). For arbitrary cameras: either derive projection directly, or transform all objects into a standard **viewing coordinate system** first.

```
Object coords --[Model]--> World coords --[View]--> View coords --[Projection]--> Clip coords --(÷w)--> NDC
```

- **Model matrix:** places object's local geometry into world space.
- **View matrix:** transforms world into the camera's frame (camera at origin).
- **Projection matrix:** perspective/orthographic, view space → clip space.

Model and view can be pre-multiplied into a model-view matrix; all three can combine into one object→clip matrix. Either may be identity.

### Look-At (constructing the view matrix)

Camera at $c$, looking at $l$, up direction $u$: forward $f=\text{normalize}(l-c)$, right $r=\text{normalize}(f\times u)$, true up $u'=r\times f$. Build rotation matrix from rows $r,u',-f$ (world axes → camera axes), combined with translation $-c$. Combined matrix: $R\cdot T(-c)$.

### Transforming normal vectors

**Pitfall:** if vertex transform $M$ is **non-orthogonal** (e.g. non-uniform scale), transforming normals by $M$ breaks perpendicularity to the surface. Since $N\cdot T=0$ must hold after transforming ($N'=GN$, $T'=MT$), requiring $N'\cdot T'=0$ gives $G^\top M=I$, so:
$$G = (M^{-1})^\top$$
Normals transform by the **inverse transpose** ("normal matrix"). For rigid transforms (orthogonal $M$), $(M^{-1})^\top=M$ — but this breaks under non-uniform scale/shear.

### Scene graph

Complex scenes are built from instances of simpler parts, each placed via a model transform relative to a parent. A **scene graph** (tree/DAG) accumulates transforms root-to-node, giving each object's world transform — enabling hierarchical animation (rotating a parent moves all children).

## 4 · Ray tracing

### Algorithm overview

For each pixel: shoot a ray from the eye through the pixel centre, find the nearest intersected object, compute its colour.

```
for each pixel (x, y):
    ray = construct_ray(eye, pixel_center(x, y))
    hit = find_nearest_intersection(ray, scene_objects)
    colour(x,y) = shade(hit) if hit else background
```

Ray tracing **easily handles** reflection, refraction, shadows, and (via distributed sampling) motion/optical blur — but is **expensive**: every pixel may test against every object, recursively.

### Ray–object intersection

- **Plane:** ray $P(t)=O+tD$, plane $N\cdot(P-P_0)=0 \Rightarrow t=\dfrac{N\cdot(P_0-O)}{N\cdot D}$.
- **Polygon/disc:** intersect with supporting plane, then test point-in-polygon.
- **Sphere:** substitute ray into $|P-C|^2=r^2$ → quadratic in $t$; smallest positive real root is nearest hit (no real roots = miss).
- **Cylinder/cone/torus:** similar (higher-degree) substitution into the implicit surface equation.

### Shading, shadows, reflection, refraction

- **Shading:** find normal at hit point, shoot **shadow rays** to each light for diffuse/specular, add ambient.
- **Shadows:** if any object lies between hit point and light, it's in shadow. **Self-shadowing**: numerical precision artefact, fixed with a small bias/epsilon offset.
- **Reflection:** spawn a recursive **reflection ray** in the mirror direction for (partially) reflective surfaces.
- **Refraction:** transparent/refractive materials bend the ray per Snell's law; combined with reflection, one ray can split into **two child rays** (reflected + refracted), recursively.

## 5 · Illumination, shading models, and the rendering equation

### Diffuse and specular reflection

Real surfaces combine **diffuse** reflection (scattered equally in all directions — gives base colour) and **specular** reflection (concentrated near the mirror direction — gives highlights). Classic example: plastic shows specular highlights in the *light's* colour, diffuse in the *plastic's* colour.

### Diffuse (Lambertian) shading

$$I_d = k_d \, I_l \, (\hat{N} \cdot \hat{L})$$

$k_d$ = diffuse reflectance/albedo (per-channel); $I_l$ = incoming light intensity. Clamp to zero if $\hat N\cdot\hat L<0$ (light behind surface). **One-sided surfaces:** only the side the normal points toward can be lit (negative cosine ⇒ black). **Two-sided surfaces:** sign of $\cos\theta$ picks which face is lit, with inverted normal for the back face.

### Specular reflection: Phong and Blinn–Phong

**Phong:** $$I_s = k_s \, I_l \, (\hat{R}\cdot \hat{V})^n$$ where $\hat R$ = ideal mirror reflection of $\hat L$ about $\hat N$, $\hat V$ = view direction, $n$ = shininess exponent (larger = tighter highlight).

**Blinn–Phong** (cheaper, better at grazing angles) uses the half-vector $\hat H=\dfrac{\hat L+\hat V}{|\hat L+\hat V|}$:
$$I_s = k_s\, I_l\, (\hat{H}\cdot\hat{N})^n$$

### Full Phong illumination equation

Per colour channel, summed over lights:
$$I = k_a I_a + \sum_{\text{lights } l} \Big[ k_d\, I_l\, (\hat N \cdot \hat L_l) + k_s\, I_l\, (\hat R_l \cdot \hat V)^n \Big]$$

$k_aI_a$ = constant ambient term approximating indirect illumination. More lights ⇒ proportionally more computation.

### Gross assumptions of this local model

Only diffuse is modelled accurately (specular is crude); **no shadows** (need ray tracing/shadow maps); **lights at infinity** (point lights need per-pixel $L$); **no inter-surface interaction** (all indirect light lumped into one ambient constant).

### The rendering equation (Kajiya)

$$L_o(x, \omega_o) = L_e(x, \omega_o) + \int_\Omega f_r(x, \omega_i, \omega_o)\, L_i(x, \omega_i)\, (\omega_i \cdot n)\, d\omega_i$$

$L_o$ = outgoing radiance, $L_e$ = emitted, $f_r$ = **BRDF** (how light scatters $\omega_i\to\omega_o$), $L_i$ = incoming radiance (recursive), $(\omega_i\cdot n)$ = Lambert cosine term, integrated over the hemisphere $\Omega$. Every physically-based renderer numerically approximates this integral; Phong/Lambertian are simple non-recursive point-sampled special cases.

## 6 · Sampling, aliasing, and anti-aliasing

### The sampling problem

A single ray through each pixel centre causes: jagged ("staircase") edges, small objects missed entirely, thin objects broken into fragments — collectively **aliasing**; mitigations are **anti-aliasing**. (Nyquist: faithful sampling needs >2× the highest frequency; sharp edges have unbounded frequency content, so some aliasing is unavoidable without pre-filtering/super-sampling.)

### Super-sampling strategies

- **Single point:** one ray per pixel centre — most aliasing.
- **Regular grid:** sub-pixel grid, one ray each — still aliases unless very fine.
- **Random:** $N$ uniformly random rays per pixel — trades aliasing for **noise** (eye less sensitive to noise).
- **Poisson disc:** $N$ random rays, no two closer than $\varepsilon$ — visually better than random for same $N$, hard to implement efficiently.
- **Jittered/stratified:** $N$ sub-pixels, one randomly-placed ray each — practical Poisson-disc approximation, easy to implement.
- **Adaptive:** shoot a few rays, check variance; stop if low, else shoot more — concentrates effort at edges/detail.

### Distributed ray tracing

Generalise "many samples per pixel": each ray samples a random value from a relevant distribution or spawns child rays.

| Distribute samples over... | Effect |
|---|---|
| Pixel area | Spatial anti-aliasing |
| Area of light source | Soft shadows |
| Camera lens aperture | Depth-of-field |
| Time | Motion blur |

## 7 · The real-time graphics pipeline and rasterisation

### Why rasterisation, not ray tracing

Ray tracing is too slow for high frame rates. Rasterisation instead: model surfaces as **polyhedra** (polygon meshes), apply perspective transform, project onto screen, determine the closest surface per pixel (hidden-surface removal), fill pixels with the nearest visible polygon's colour. GPUs have dedicated rasterisation hardware; accelerated ray tracing exists but is less efficient for most workloads.

### Meshes and triangulation

3D objects = **polygonal meshes**; curved surfaces are tessellated into polygons. A **triangle** (3 vertices) is guaranteed planar; larger polygons aren't. GPUs are optimised for triangles, so polygons are **split into triangles**, avoiding extra/elongated triangles (expensive, numerically unstable).

### Rasterisation vs ray tracing — loop structure

| | Outer loop | Inner loop |
|---|---|---|
| **Ray tracing** | every pixel | every object/triangle |
| **Rasterisation** | every triangle | every covered fragment |

```
for each triangle T:
    project T's vertices to screen space; compute bounding box
    for each pixel (x, y) in bounding box:
        compute barycentric coords (alpha, beta, gamma) of (x, y)
        if alpha >= 0 and beta >= 0 and gamma >= 0:   # inside
            interpolate attributes; shade and write fragment (depth test)
```

### Barycentric coordinates

A point $(x,y)$ in triangle $A,B,C$ is $\alpha A+\beta B+\gamma C$ with $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1$ (exactly 1 at the corresponding vertex, 0 at others). Using the implicit edge-line equation $f_{PQ}(x,y)=(y_P-y_Q)x+(x_Q-x_P)y+x_Py_Q-x_Qy_P$:

$$\alpha = \frac{f_{cb}(x,y)}{f_{cb}(x_a,y_a)}, \qquad \beta = \frac{f_{ac}(x,y)}{f_{ac}(x_b,y_b)}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{f_{ab}(x,y)}{f_{ab}(x_c,y_c)}$$

Each is the opposite edge's line equation, normalised to give 1 at its vertex and 0 along the opposite edge. **Inside test:** $\alpha,\beta,\gamma \ge 0$. **Attribute interpolation:** $v = \alpha v_a + \beta v_b + \gamma v_c$ (colour, normal, depth, UV, ...).

### Triangle rasterisation algorithm

1. Compute screen-space bounding box ($x_{\min},x_{\max},y_{\min},y_{\max}$).
2. For each pixel in the box, compute $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$; if all $\ge 0$, interpolate and write the fragment; else skip.

**Incremental optimisation:** $f_{PQ}$ is affine, so $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ change by a **constant** when stepping one pixel right/down — precompute $\Delta\alpha,\Delta\beta,\Delta\gamma$ and update incrementally while scanning. Heavily optimised in GPU hardware, enabling millions of triangles/frame.

### Shading during rasterisation

Per-vertex colours/normals (e.g. via Phong) are **interpolated** across fragments via barycentric weights; quality improves with polygon count. **Normal interpolation + per-pixel shading:** if the full Phong (incl. specular) is evaluated per-vertex and interpolated, highlights are either missed or smeared unrealistically. Modern GPUs interpolate **normals** (cheap) and compute the **specular term per fragment** (expensive but realistic); diffuse looks acceptable either way.

## 8 · Hidden surface removal: the Z-buffer

### Buffers and the algorithm

- **Colour buffer:** rendered image. **Depth (Z) buffer:** nearest depth written so far, per pixel.

```
initialise colour buffer to background, depth buffer to z_max
for each triangle (any order):
    for each fragment (x, y, z):
        if z < depth_buffer[x, y]:
            depth_buffer[x, y] = z; colour_buffer[x, y] = shade(fragment)
        # else: occluded, discard
```

$z_{\max}$ = far clipping plane; $z_{\min}$ = near clipping plane. **Inefficiency:** far-to-near processing causes **overdraw** (repeated shading of overwritten fragments) — mitigate by sorting front-to-back or early-Z/occlusion culling.

### Precision: near/far planes and z-fighting

Depth buffer has **limited precision** (24/32-bit, often storing $1/z$). **Far plane** bounds an otherwise-infinite range; **near plane** keeps $z$ away from 0 (since $1/z\to\infty$ as $z\to0$). Practical symptom: near-plane clipping makes close geometry disappear. **Z-fighting:** shimmering when two triangles have nearly-equal $z$ — floating-point rounding randomly picks the depth-test "winner" each frame.

### Other hidden-surface methods

- **Painter's algorithm:** render back-to-front by sorted depth — fails on cyclic overlaps.
- **BSP tree:** precomputed spatial partition giving a valid back-to-front order for any viewpoint — resolves cyclic overlap, costs preprocessing.
- **Z-buffer:** dominant approach — simple, per-pixel, hardware-supported, handles arbitrary order/cyclic overlap (at the cost of memory and overdraw).

## 9 · GPUs and graphics APIs

### What is a GPU?

A **GPU** is a CPU specialised for floating-point ops on **large arrays** (vertices, normals, pixels) — handling clipping, rasterisation, hidden-surface removal, plus procedural shading/texturing/animation, RT acceleration structures, video encode/decode, physics. **Fully programmable** at several stages but optimised for **massive parallelism**: hundreds/thousands of SIMD processors, memory bandwidth ≫1000 GB/s (for favourable access patterns) — 3D rendering parallelises well since per-vertex/fragment ops are mostly independent.

### Graphics and compute APIs

| API | Platform | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **OpenGL** | Cross-platform, open | General 3D; driver manages resources; no native ray-tracing |
| **DirectX** | Windows/Xbox, Microsoft | Games-focused; app manages resources |
| **Vulkan** | Cross-platform, open | Low-overhead, fine GPU control, multi-core; even simple draws need **thousands of lines**; for heavily-optimised engines |
| **Metal** | Apple (iOS 8+) | Low-level 3D+compute; Swift/Obj-C; Vulkan-like philosophy |
| **OpenGL ES** | Mobile | Stripped-down OpenGL |
| **WebGL** (~2007) | Browser | JS, based on OpenGL ES |
| **WebGPU** (~2017) | Browser | Vulkan/Metal/DirectX access; WGSL shading language |

**GPGPU:** graphics APIs aren't ideal for general compute (physics, ML). **CUDA** (Nvidia-only, C-like with parallel APIs) vs **OpenCL** (open standard, GPUs+some CPUs, generally less fast/supported than CUDA).

### OpenGL programming model and pipeline

- **CPU side** (`gl*` functions): create objects, copy data CPU↔GPU, modify state, enqueue rendering, synchronise.
- **GPU side:** shaders (vertex, fragment, ...) written in **GLSL**.

```
Vertex data --> [Vertex Shader] --> [Tessellation Ctrl/Eval] --> [Geometry Shader]
            --> [Primitive Assembly] --> [Clipping] --> [Rasterisation] --> [Fragment Shader] --> Framebuffer
```

- **Vertex Shader:** per-vertex (position, normal, UV); runs once per vertex.
- **Tessellation:** subdivides patches for adaptive LOD.
- **Geometry Shader:** creates new primitives from existing ones.
- **Primitive Assembly:** groups vertices into points/lines/triangles.
- **Clipping:** keeps geometry within the view frustum.
- **Rasterisation:** generates fragments, barycentric-interpolates attributes (§7).
- **Fragment Shader:** final per-fragment colour — can sample textures, modify depth, tone-map.

### Preparing vertex data — cube example

1. Split each square face into two triangles; assign 3D positions per vertex.
2. Assign **normals**: a cube needs **more normals than naive sharing suggests** — if adjacent faces shared a vertex+normal, OpenGL would interpolate between differing face normals, rounding the corners. So positionally-identical vertices on different faces are **duplicated** with distinct normals.
3. Build a **vertex attribute array** (index, position, normal) and a separate **index array** (triangles as index triples).
4. **Winding order:** specify triangle vertices consistently (conventionally **anticlockwise**, ACW, front-facing) so OpenGL can compute face-normal direction via cross product and **backface-cull** away-facing polygons.

## 10 · GLSL fundamentals

GLSL is C/Java-like: primitive/aggregate types, structs, arrays, vector/matrix arithmetic, control flow (`if`, `switch`, `for`, `while`), functions — small programs run per-vertex/per-fragment.

```glsl
#version 330 core
layout(location = 0) in vec3 pos;     // vertex position, local space
layout(location = 1) in vec3 normal;  // vertex normal, local space
uniform mat4 mvp;                     // model-view-projection matrix
out vec3 vN;                          // normal passed to fragment shader

void main() {
  gl_Position = mvp * vec4(pos, 1.0);  // project to clip space
  vN = normal;  // normally transformed by inverse-transpose of model matrix
}
```

### Data types

- **Basic:** `float`, `double`, `int`, `uint`, `bool`
- **Aggregate:** `vec2..4`/`mat2..4` (float), `dvec`/`dmat` (double), `ivec` (int), `uvec` (uint), `bvec` (bool)

### Indexing and swizzling

Vector components via `rgba`, `xyzw`, `stpq`, or 0-based index — all equivalent (`color.r == color.x == color[0]`; `M[1][1]` indexes a matrix). **Swizzling** selects/reorders multiple components:

```glsl
vec4 rgba_color = vec4(1.0, 1.0, 0.0, 1.0);
vec3 rgb_color  = rgba_color.rgb;   // (1.0, 1.0, 0.0)
vec3 bgr_color  = rgba_color.bgr;   // (0.0, 1.0, 1.0)
vec3 grayscale  = rgba_color.ggg;   // repeat a component
```

### Arrays and storage qualifiers

```glsl
float lut[5] = float[5](1.0, 1.42, 1.73, 2.0, 2.23);
for (int i = 0; i < lut.length(); i++) { lut[i] *= 2; }
```

| Qualifier | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `const` | Read-only, compile-time |
| `in` / `out` | Shader input / output |
| `uniform` | App-supplied parameter, constant per draw call |
| `buffer` | App-allocated GPU memory, read/write |
| `shared` | Shared within a work group (compute shaders) |

Supports arithmetic/bitwise/logical/assignment operators plus a maths library: trig, exponential, `min`/`max`/`clamp`/`mix`, and graphics functions `normalize`/`reflect`/`refract`/`dot`/`cross`.

## 11 · Textures

### Texture mapping pipeline

1. Define texture function $T(u,v)$, $(u,v)\in[0,1]$.
2. Define a vertex ↔ $(u,v)$ correspondence ("UV mapping"), authored once per mesh.
3. When rendering, interpolate $(u,v)$ barycentrically, look up $T(u,v)$, use as colour/reflectance (e.g. feeding $k_d$).

### Filtering: up- vs down-sampling

- **Up-sampling (magnification):** texture lower-res than screen area — interpolate between texels. **Nearest-neighbour** (blocky) vs **bilinear** (weighted avg of 4 nearest texels, smoother).
- **Down-sampling (minification):** texture higher-res than screen area — naive point-sampling aliases badly. **Area averaging** over a pixel's footprint avoids this, especially for high-frequency textures at distance/grazing angles.

### Mipmaps

A **mipmap** pyramid stores the texture at multiple resolutions, each level half the size (power-of-2 dimensions) — a pre-filtered, area-averaged texture for minification; the renderer picks/interpolates ("trilinear") the best-matching level. Full chain costs only **1/3 extra** storage; OpenGL can generate mipmaps automatically. **Anisotropic filtering:** extends mipmapping to oblique viewing angles where the footprint stretches more in one direction, sampling across multiple levels/directions to avoid blur in one axis and aliasing in the other.

### Tiling, multi-surface UV maps, bump/displacement/environment mapping

- **Tiling:** small repeating tiles, with texture coordinates wrapping ($T(1.1,0)=T(0.1,0)$).
- **Atlas:** one texture image used across multiple surfaces/objects, each mapping to a different region.
- **Bump/normal mapping:** a texture perturbs the **shading normal** without changing geometry — cheap, fragment-shader only.
- **Displacement mapping:** like bump mapping but actually **modifies geometry/position** — correct silhouettes/self-occlusion, but needs tessellation/geometry shaders.
- **Environment mapping:** reflects the surroundings assuming they're **infinitely far away** (reflection depends only on direction). Implemented via a precomputed environment texture, e.g. a **cube map** (six faces). Fast, convincing but not physically exact approximation of mirror reflections.

## 12 · Buffers, double buffering, and display sync

### Render buffer types (OpenGL)

- **Colour buffer(s):** rendered image. **Depth buffer:** nearest-fragment tracking for Z-buffer. **Stencil buffer:** masks pixels from drawing (e.g. UI overlays already covered) — also enables **planar mirrors** (re-render scene from a reflected camera, restricted via stencil to the mirror's pixels). **Stereo buffers:** separate left/right-eye buffers — rarely used.

### Double/triple buffering

**Front buffer** (displayed) + **back buffer** (GPU draws here); a cheap pointer **swap** exchanges roles once drawing finishes — avoids showing partially-drawn frames. **Triple buffering** adds a third buffer so the GPU needn't idle waiting for a swap; costs more memory and higher latency.

### V-Sync, tearing, and adaptive sync

The display copies the front buffer line-by-line; a mid-scan swap causes **tearing** (top = old frame, bottom = new). **V-Sync** defers the swap to the end of the scan, guaranteeing one consistent frame — but if drawing takes longer than the refresh interval, the previous frame repeats (stutter/frame drop).

**FreeSync (AMD) / G-Sync (Nvidia)** implement **Variable Refresh Rate**: the GPU signals the display when a frame is ready and the display adapts its timing, rather than the display dictating a fixed rate. Benefits: power savings on static content, reduced input lag vs fixed V-Sync.

## 13 · Human vision and colour science

### Eye anatomy, rods and cones

**Cornea/lens** focus light; **pupil** controls light entry; **retina** holds photoreceptors; **fovea** (centre) has highest cone density, no rods, sharpest vision; **optic nerve** carries signals to the visual cortex.

**Rods:** night/low-light vision, no colour, absent from fovea. **Cones:** daylight + colour, three types sensitive to short/medium/long wavelengths — called **LMS** (not RGB) because sensitivities are broad, overlapping, and L/M are very close (evolutionarily recent split).

Visible light spans roughly **360–730 nm** (~380–780 nm also commonly cited); higher-energy than thermal IR, so ambient heat doesn't interfere with vision.

### Reflectance, cone responses, and metamers

Light reaching the eye = illuminant × surface reflectance: $L(\lambda) = I(\lambda)\,R(\lambda)$. A "red" object can look dark green if the eye is much less sensitive to red than green at the relevant intensities — perception depends on $L(\lambda)$ combined with LMS sensitivities, not $R(\lambda)$ alone. RGB-only reflectance is a crude approximation to true spectral reflectance (full-spectrum is expensive/hard to acquire).

$$\text{cone response} = \sum_\lambda \big(\text{sensitivity}(\lambda) \times \text{reflected light}(\lambda)\big)$$

This projects the infinite-dimensional spectrum to **three** numbers (L,M,S). **Metamers:** different spectra producing identical LMS responses look identical — why displays reproduce convincing colour with just three RGB primaries (a metameric match, not a spectral match).

### Tristimulus theory and CIE XYZ

**Tristimulus theory:** any colour is matched by a mixture of **three** primaries (sometimes requiring "negative" contributions, handled experimentally by adding to the test side). **Colour-matching functions** give, for monochromatic colours, how much of each primary matches.

**CIE XYZ:** CIE 1931 (Guild & Wright, ~12 observers, 2° foveal field) gives the original XYZ matching functions; CIE 2006 derives from LMS (Stockman & Sharpe), differing most in S-cone response. XYZ uses **standardised, imaginary primaries** matching all realisable stimuli, chosen so all matching functions are **positive** and $Y\propto$ **luminance**. LMS is a linear transform of XYZ.

### CIE chromaticity diagram

Discards luminance: $x=\frac{X}{X+Y+Z}$, $y=\frac{Y}{X+Y+Z}$, $z=\frac{Z}{X+Y+Z}$, $x+y+z=1$ (so only $x,y$ needed). Spectral colours lie on the curved **spectral locus**; all perceivable colours lie inside; outside = not physically realisable.

### Opponent processing, luminance, gamuts

LMS signals are re-encoded by **colour-opponent mechanisms**: achromatic $(L+M)$ → brightness/luminance; chromatic $(L-M)$ → red-green; chromatic $(S-(L+M))$ → blue-yellow. Rods feed the same pathways (no separate channel), dominating at low light, cones at high light, both in between.

**Luminance:** light weighted by the achromatic mechanism, units cd/m² (**nit**) — not the same as "perceived brightness".

**Gamuts/HDR vs SDR:** all visible colours form a 3D solid in XYZ; a display reproduces only a sub-region (**gamut**). A chromaticity diagram is a 2D slice; the full solid (chromaticity + $\log Y$) tapers toward dark, as cones desensitise and the gamut shrinks to a rod-only line. **HDR** = scene-referred, aims to capture/reproduce nearly all visible colours, display does final tone-mapping. **SDR** = display-referred, sRGB gamut, mimics CRT.

## 14 · Display encoding and colour representation

### Gamma correction

Rendering produces **linear** values (proportional to physical light), but displays expect encoded integers (0–255). A **display encoding** (OETF) maps linear → encoded; the display's **EOTF** inverts it. Gamma is the standard SDR encoding:
$$V_{out} = V_{in}^{\gamma} \quad (\text{decoding}), \qquad V_{out} = V_{in}^{1/\gamma} \quad (\text{encoding})$$
with $\gamma\approx 2.2$, historically modelling CRT voltage-to-light response.

### Why gamma is needed

Linear luminance is **perceptually non-uniform** (0→0.1 is a huge jump; 0.8→0.9 barely noticeable). Gamma-encoded values are distributed more **perceptually uniformly**. Practically: without gamma, ≥12 bits/channel would be needed to avoid banding (vs 8 bits with it).

### Luma vs luminance

**Luma** $L'$ (weighted sum of gamma-encoded RGB, used as a grayscale proxy in editing):
$$L' = 0.2126\,R' + 0.7152\,G' + 0.0722\,B'$$

**(Relative) luminance** $L$ (true physical quantity, weighted sum of **linear** RGB):
$$L = 0.2126\,R + 0.7152\,G + 0.0722\,B = 0.2126\,(R')^{\gamma} + 0.7152\,(G')^{\gamma} + 0.0722\,(B')^{\gamma}$$

Despite similar formulas, luminance requires converting back to linear (raise to $\gamma$) first — a common mistake is treating gamma-encoded RGB as already linear.

### sRGB, HDR standards, and sRGB textures in OpenGL

**sRGB** ≈ gamma 2.2, differing slightly near black (small linear segment avoids an infinite gradient at zero). HDR formats use wider gamuts (e.g. BT.2020) and different transfer functions (e.g. **PQ**, Perceptual Quantizer) for higher peak luminance.

OpenGL **sRGB textures** store gamma-encoded data; on lookup/filtering OpenGL converts to **linear** automatically (filtering is only correct in linear space); writing to an sRGB framebuffer converts linear shader output back to sRGB. **Common bug:** sampling an sRGB texture as linear gives shading that's too dark/contrasty.

### Converting between colour spaces

General pipeline: (1) decode display encoding → linear RGB; (2) linear RGB → **XYZ** via a fixed 3×3 matrix; (3) XYZ → target linear RGB (e.g. BT.2020) via another 3×3 matrix; (4) apply target encoding (e.g. PQ). Consecutive matrix steps (2)–(3) can be **pre-multiplied** into one matrix. General principle: **all colour conversions happen in linear space**; encode/decode only at input/output boundaries.

### Choosing a colour representation

- **Compact storage:** display-encoded RGB (sRGB) — framebuffers, textures.
- **Human-friendly selection:** Munsell, HSV, HLS.
- **Perceptually uniform (Euclidean ≈ perceived difference):** CIE Lab, CIE Luv.

**CMY/CMYK (subtractive):** inks absorb light (opposite of displays' emission) — cyan absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue; CMY ≈ inverse RGB. **CMYK** adds black ("Key") because real inks absorb imperfectly, C+M+Y mixes to muddy brown not true black, and aligning three inks for pure black text is impractical.

**Munsell** (Hue, Value, Chroma): perceptually-motivated artist's system, empirically equal perceptual steps, but **highly irregular** geometry (vivid yellow much brighter than vivid blue at same Value). **HSV** (Hue, Saturation, Value): same goals via a simple RGB transform — geometrically an inverted hexagonal pyramid (apex black, base centre white, hue = angle, value = axis). **HLS** (Hue, Lightness, Saturation): fixes HSV's flaw that white and saturated colours share the same "Value" by adding a Lightness axis stretching white upward.

**Perceptual uniformity, Luv/Lab:** CIE $xy$ chromaticity is very non-uniform — **MacAdam ellipses** (measured discrimination thresholds) vary wildly in size across the diagram. **CIE $u'v'$** is a transform making these ellipses more uniform/circular. **CIE L\*u\*v\*** = $u'v'$ chromaticity + Lightness $L^*$ axis (HSV-like but perceptually uniform); **CIE L\*a\*b\*** is an alternative with similar goals — neither is conclusively better, and even these aren't perfectly uniform (differences shrink near black/white, max chroma varies by hue).

## 15 · Tone mapping

### The problem and goals

Rendered/HDR images can exceed a display's **dynamic range**. **Tone mapping** compresses the source range to fit. Goals: reduce dynamic range, customise look (grading), simulate vision (scotopic) or camera response, adapt to display/viewing conditions, increase realism, and primarily map **scene-referred → display-referred** colours.

### Tone curves

A function mapping input (scene) luminance to output (display) luminance. **Identity** would be ideal but impossible (limited peak brightness/black level). A naive **linear rescale** squeezes the whole range in, ignoring content. Better: **adapt the curve to content** — steep where pixel values cluster (preserve contrast), and **clip** rare extreme values (less perceptually important).

### Sigmoidal (photographic / Reinhard) operator

Digital cameras mimic film's S-shaped response: **toe** (dark end, smooth saturation), **linear** central region (typical exposure), **shoulder** (bright end, smooth saturation) — fast and gives a pleasing photographic look.

For each linear channel value $L$:
$$L_{out} = \frac{L^{b}}{L^{b} + a}$$

- $a$ shifts the curve (often anchored to the image's geometric-mean/log-average luminance $L_m$) → brightens/darkens.
- $b$ controls steepness/contrast: larger $b$ → higher contrast but **narrower** reproducible range (faster clipping); smaller $b$ → lower contrast but **wider** range without clipping.

This is the fundamental **contrast vs dynamic-range trade-off**: no operator maximises both on a limited display.

### Other named operators

- **Linear scaling:** multiply by a constant so a reference maps to white; clips everything brighter.
- **Drago:** logarithmic operator adapting its base to the image's max luminance, preserving detail across a wide range.
- **ACES:** industry-standard tone-mapping/colour pipeline for consistent filmic response (film/games).

## Common pitfalls

- **Matrix order matters:** $(model)(view)(projection)$ on a column vector ≠ $(projection)(view)(model)$ — rightmost matrix applies first (Cambridge convention).
- **Forgetting the perspective divide:** clip space ($w\ne1$) ≠ NDC — must divide by $w$.
- **Non-orthogonal transforms break normals:** use $(M^{-1})^\top$, not $M$, under non-uniform scale/shear.
- **Transformations don't commute:** check order in both 2D and 3D.
- **Barycentric coordinates sum to 1**, each in $[0,1]$ inside the triangle — use for both inside-test and interpolation.
- **Z-buffer precision worsens far from the camera** (especially storing $1/z$) — poor near/far ratios cause z-fighting.
- **Specular highlights need per-pixel evaluation** — per-vertex Phong + interpolation misses or smears highlights.
- **sRGB/gamma must be handled correctly:** convert sRGB textures to linear on input, linear shader output back to sRGB on output.
- **Luma ≠ luminance:** luminance needs converting gamma-encoded values back to linear first.
- **Mipmaps are essential at minification**, not optional — without them, distant/grazing high-frequency textures alias badly.
- **CIE XYZ is device-independent; RGB/sRGB is not** — RGB is tied to specific primaries + transfer function; any RGB space converts to/from XYZ via a 3×3 matrix.
- **Tone mapping trades contrast for dynamic range** — $a,b$ (or equivalents) always involve this trade-off.

## Exam strategy

- **Pipeline questions:** sketch the full MVP pipeline (object → world → view → clip → NDC → screen), marking where the perspective divide happens.
- **Shading questions:** write the **full** Phong equation, identify every term ($k_aI_a$, diffuse, specular, $\hat N,\hat L,\hat R/\hat H,\hat V$), and state the gross assumptions (no shadows, lights at infinity, no inter-reflection).
- **Projection questions:** derive $x'=dx/z$ from similar triangles, then show the matrix + perspective-divide form.
- **Rasterisation questions:** define barycentric coordinates via implicit line equations, show the inside-test, mention $\Delta\alpha,\Delta\beta,\Delta\gamma$ incremental optimisation if efficiency is asked.
- **Colour questions:** explain why XYZ is device-independent (fixed imaginary primaries) vs RGB/sRGB (tied to display primaries/transfer function), and state luma vs luminance precisely.
- **Tone mapping questions:** state the goals (scene→display-referred, dynamic range reduction), give the Reinhard formula, discuss the $a$/$b$ contrast-vs-range trade-off.
- **Z-buffer questions:** explain why near/far planes are needed (depth precision, especially $1/z$) and describe z-fighting.
- **GLSL/OpenGL questions:** be ready to write a short vertex/fragment shader — know `in`/`out`/`uniform` and `gl_Position = mvp * vec4(pos, 1.0)`.
