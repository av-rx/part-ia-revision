## 1 · Foundations: IxD and user-centred design

### Goals of interaction design

- Develop **interactive** products — UX as well as UI.
- Develop **usable** products — help users do something / fulfil tasks.
- Alternative approaches besides UCD: Participatory Design / co-design, Contextual Design, etc.

### Why take a UCD approach

- Useful software sells better.
- "You aren't your user" — in most cases the designer has never met the user.
- A structured way of allowing yourself to be **surprised**.

UCD helps with:
- **Building the right software** — useful purpose.
- **Building the software right** — usable for its intended purpose.

$\Rightarrow$ UCD is a structured way of building **empathy** for the people affected by the software.

### UCD as part of the software engineering process

- Identifying target users / stakeholders.
- Developing requirements.
- Iterative/agile development — UCD methods slot into the iterative cycle; people find it hard to say what they want without a concrete artefact to react to.
- **Return on investment:** the cost of changes rises roughly **10×** per phase. Building the wrong thing means rebuilding from scratch — correcting misunderstandings early saves enormous cost.

### The UCD cycle

1. **Understand context of use** — who, what, where, why.
2. **Specify user requirements** — derived from research.
3. **Produce design solutions** — sketches, wireframes, prototypes.
4. **Evaluate against requirements** — usability test, expert review.

Cycle until requirements are met.

### Types of context for design

| Context | Characteristics | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumer apps** (discretionary) | User can stop using at any time | "Walk up and use" usability |
| **Enterprise / government apps** (compulsory) | User compelled to use through work; training possible | Productivity |
| **Socio-technical services** (mixed) | Provision of a service users may or may not take up | No strong a-priori usability priority |

## 2 · User research methods

Research is **empirical** — collects quantitative (numbers) and qualitative (words/images/video) data. Common challenges: recruitment, ethics and risk of distorting data, researcher/participant power disparity, confidentiality/anonymity, incentives.

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Unstructured interview** | Rich, interviewee-led data | Too much data, off-target |
| **Semi-structured interview** | Rich AND targeted | Still a lot of data |
| **Structured interview** | Straightforward to collect/compare | Rigid |
| **Focus groups (different stakeholder groups)** | Surfaces tensions between groups | Conflict |
| **Focus groups (same stakeholder group)** | More efficient than individual interviews | Dominant participants / groupthink |
| **Observation — field (ethnography)** | Realistic setting, authentic behaviour | Hard to set up, intrusive, privacy issues, Hawthorne effect |
| **Observation — lab** | Less intrusive | Not a realistic setting |
| **Direct + concurrent think-aloud** | Good for understanding context as it happens | Lots of data, time-consuming |
| **Indirect + retrospective protocol** | Good for cognitively demanding tasks | Users forget, time-consuming |
| **Questionnaires** | Reach many people, qual + quant | Rigid, impersonal |
| **Card sorting** | Reveals terminology/relationships/categories | — |
| **Studying documentation** | Available/accessible, no stakeholder time needed | Idealised, outdated, insufficient alone |
| **Researching similar products** | Prompts requirements, alternative designs | May inhibit creativity |
| **Analytics / clickstream** | Real, large-scale | Lacks the "why" |
| **Diary studies** | Longitudinal | Requires participant discipline |

### Interview types in detail

- **Unstructured** (no prepared questions) — interviewee-led, rich data; risks too much off-target data.
- **Semi-structured** (some prepared questions) — rich and targeted, but still produces a lot of data.
- **Structured** (all questions prepared) — straightforward to collect but rigid.

Guidelines: focus on **concrete examples**, ask **open** questions, avoid **leading** questions and jargon.

### Questionnaire question types

- **Open questions** — respondent answers freely.
- **Closed questions** — numerical input, simple checklist (yes/no/don't know), multi-point rating scale, ranked order.

### Card sorting variants

- **Open** — no pre-established groups; participants sort and label freely.
- **Closed** — pre-established groups provided; participants assign cards to them.
- **Hybrid** — combination of both.

### Mixed methods designs

| Design | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **Triangulation** | Collect QUAL + QUANT simultaneously, interpret together |
| **Embedded** | One method nested within the other (e.g. QUANT with embedded qual) |
| **Explanatory** | QUANT first, then QUAL to explain results |
| **Exploratory** | QUAL first, then QUANT to test/generalise findings |

### Choosing methods

Depends on: available time, risk, researcher knowledge, design context, and the nature of user tasks (sequential vs overlapping, simple vs complex, layman vs expert).

### Key guidelines for all research

- Focus on users' and stakeholders' needs.
- Involve **all** stakeholder groups (more than one representative each).
- Get **informed consent**.
- Use **mixed methods** — combine approaches and data types.
- **Plan analysis before collecting data.**
- Iterate on requirements and designs between data-collection activities.

## 3 · Requirements analysis

### What is a requirement

> *"Something that the product must do or a quality that the product must have."* (Robertson & Robertson, 1999)

A requirements specification answers: what should the software do to meet user needs; what properties/attributes should the system have; what constraints apply to development?

**Why it matters:** without requirements analysis, customers explain needs ambiguously, designers misunderstand wishes, and programmers implement the wrong thing — failures here are **expensive and slow** to fix later. Requirements let the team agree *what* the system will do and *how* before implementation.

### Functional vs non-functional

- **Functional** — what the system should do.
- **Non-functional** — constraints on the system/development: data requirements, environmental requirements, user characteristics, usability goals & UX requirements.

This split is **imprecise** and falsely implies functional requirements matter more.

### Wiegers & Beatty hierarchy

A richer hierarchy flowing from business goals down to specification:

**Business Requirements** → Vision & Scope Document → **User Requirements** → User Requirements Document → **Functional Requirements** → Software Requirements Specification

**Business Rules** influence all levels; **Quality Attributes**, **External Interfaces**, and **Constraints** feed into the SRS.

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Business Requirement** | High-level objectives of the org/customer. *Why* are we building this? |
| **Business Rule** | Policy/guideline/standard/regulation constraining the business — origin of several requirements, not itself a software requirement |
| **User/Stakeholder Requirement** | Goal or task users must perform — captured via use cases, scenarios, user stories |
| **Quality Requirement** | Non-functional — *how well* the system performs: usability, performance, reliability, maintainability, security, portability, interoperability |
| **Functional Requirement** | A behaviour exhibited under specific conditions — inputs, outputs, functionality, data manipulated (a.k.a. *solution requirement*) |
| **System Requirement** | Top-level requirement for a multi-system product (software, or software + hardware) |
| **External Interface Requirement** | A connection to the outside world — user, other system, or hardware |
| **Constraint** | Restriction on developer choices — implementation/delivery method, technology, dev environment, legacy systems, existing DBs |

**Mapping to the dev process:**
- *What to build, for whom, why?* → Business Requirements
- *What features matter most?* → User/Stakeholder Requirements
- *How should each feature work?* → Functional Requirements
- *Is it usable?* → Quality Requirements

### Worked example: hiking app

- **User Requirement:** maintain an accurate UK map with hiking-safety info, allow creating/storing hike plans, track via GPS, warn user if off-course.
- **Functional/Solution Requirements** (one decomposition): store UK map with roads/footpaths/terrain/elevation with toggleable satellite imagery; display Met Office weather warnings on the map; check for map updates weekly; settings page lets the user choose auto-update vs manual permission.
- **Constraint:** mobile app, both Android & iOS.
- **Reliability:** works offline during tracking mode.
- **Usability:** onboarding flow < 10 steps.

> Design/prototyping bridges **User Requirements → Functional Requirements**; implementation realises the **SRS**.

## 4 · Exploring the design space

### One requirement, many designs

The same user requirements can be implemented many ways (e.g. audiobook apps — Audible, Google Books, LibriVox all differ in layout). Constraints and quality attributes prune the space, but infinite possibilities remain.

### UCD approach to design

Create and compare alternative designs while:
1. **Keeping the user in mind** — improve the user's environment and goals, not just system requirements.
2. **Being iterative**: $\text{design} \to \text{measure (against requirements) / test (with users)} \to \text{redesign}$.

### Methods overview

Brainstorming, moodboarding, concept development, design principles, storyboards, and prototyping — mix and match as the project demands.

### Brainstorming

Generates ideas to solve design problems. One person facilitates; house rules:
- Set a time limit.
- Begin with a target problem/brief.
- Refrain from judgment/criticism.
- Encourage weird and wacky ideas.
- Aim for **quantity**.
- Build on others' ideas.

**Structuring team interaction:**
- **Group** — individuals contribute directly (e.g. Osborn's method).
- **Individual → Group** — brainstorm alone then share (nominal group technique, team idea mapping).
- **Individual ↔ Individual → Group** — respond to others' ideas then discuss as a group (group passing technique, directed brainstorming).

**Structuring idea generation:** brain-netting (no structure), mind mapping (associations/relationships), starbursting (what/where/when/why/how per idea), gap analysis, SWOT analysis.

**Challenges:** peer pressure, groupthink, disengagement, dominant voices. Mitigations: anonymous idea collection, clear facilitator role, time-boxing, individual-first techniques.

### Moodboard

A **collage** giving the "feel" of the design — images, text, samples in a chosen composition. Used to develop design concepts/style guides and communicate to the team. Feeds into (but is distinct from) the **style guide** (typography, colour palette, components) → final UI. Different personas drive radically different moodboards (commuter → calm/escapist; teen skater → bold colours; retiree → cozy/literary).

**Challenges:** must match branding while supporting usability/UX; easy for visuals, harder for interaction; little tool support; subjective and hard to critique ("the feel of it is not quite right").

### Concept development

Identifies a **driving concept or metaphor** that drives both **graphic** decisions (layout, colours, fonts, imagery) and **interaction** decisions (UI elements, widgets, animation, navigation). A strong concept produces a **coherent** design.

- **Verbal concepts** — words describing the interface; abstract; focus on the *message*.
- **Visual concepts** — images/colour schemes; concrete; focus on *how* the message is conveyed.

Brainstorming and moodboarding both feed concept creation.

**Examples:** the **desktop metaphor** (Apple Macintosh System 1 — folders, files, trash); Microsoft OneNote's **notebook metaphor** (sections as coloured tabs, pages within sections, drawing as a key input modality).

**Challenges:** must match branding while supporting UX; a concept implemented too literally can be distracting; getting team agreement on a common vision.

### Product-specific design principles

Value statements that frame and guide design decisions — distinct from generic universal/platform principles. Used to focus design around user values, build a **consistent** experience, get **team alignment**, fuel creativity, and make faster decisions.

**Examples:**
- **Medium** (Dustin Senos): *Direction over Choice* (limit layout/type/colour choices to keep focus on writing); *Appropriate over Consistent* (break consistency when OS/device/context warrants it); *Evolving over Finalized* (drafts, responses, notes improve with use).
- **Firefox Design Values:** Takes care of you, Plays well with others, Exuberance, Finely crafted, Global, Balances power and simplicity, Makes sense of the web, High user-performance, You help make it.

**Where values are used (Firefox model):** (1) filter & choose which design opportunities to pursue, (2) shape design decisions during refine & test, (3) guide implementation trade-offs and what's shipped, (4) establish what success looks like.

**Guidelines for good principles:** small in number (3–5, memorable); differentiating (not just generic usability truisms — explain why a user picks *you*); unambiguous and actionable; concise and memorable.

**Challenges:** too generic/not opinionated; don't actually help (or even interfere if too rigid); prioritising which matter most.

### Storyboards

A rough representation of a user's activities as a **series of frames/drawings** showing a sequential storyline of how a user progresses through activities. Used to force thinking about user flow/tasks, iterate quickly, and communicate what users (can) do and how.

**Two foci:**
- **Context around interaction** — sketches of the user in their environment with thoughts/emotions.
- **Screen & interaction flow** — wireframes connected by transitions/annotations.

**How to create:** (1) consider a persona and scenario (context), (2) think of the sequence of actions, (3) add detail toward a solution, (4) write the story, (5) draw the storyboard, (6) caption each frame.

**Challenges:** wrong level of detail (must show interaction, not just screens); cost (good storyboards take time and revision); version management as features change.

### Prototyping — overview

> An incomplete, early version of a product — an interactive version of a design, not production-grade. The best way to evaluate alternative designs is letting users interact with them (see Wizard of Oz, §9).

**Why prototype:**
- **Sketch** — explore the design space (interaction, form, technology, concepts, content).
- **Demo** — to team, client, user, funder.
- **Develop design** — test concepts/assumptions; "thinking through making"; identify and prioritise key features; choose between alternatives.
- **Evaluation/user testing** — objective feedback (designer is biased), real usage, find opportunities and problems.

**What to prototype:** technical aspects, workflow/task design, screen layouts & info display, graphic design/look-and-feel, content (taxonomy of objects/widgets), controversial/critical areas (security, privacy).

**Kinds — by polish:** low fidelity (lo-fi) vs high fidelity (hi-fi).
**Kinds — by interactivity:** non-interactive (storyboards, wireframes) vs interactive (click-through — Adobe XD, Balsamiq, PowerPoint with transitions — or coded).

| Kind | Characteristics | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Paper** | Drawing/diagram/photoshop; quick, cheap, sketchy; many iterations | Early exploration, lo-fi user testing |
| **Wireframe** | Schematic of IA; layout only, no visual design; shows content/nav relationships | Structuring screens before visual design |
| **Video** | Quick or complex; sketchy or polished; tells a story | Demo impossible/costly — speculative tech, future scenarios (e.g. *Beyond the Fold*) |
| **Form model** | Doesn't work but shows form; rough or refined | Shows how the object exists in the world; allows direct handling (e.g. Palm Pilot wooden block) |

## 5 · Prototyping fidelity in depth

### Fidelity continuum

| Fidelity | Examples | Cost | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sketches | pencil, whiteboard | minutes | brainstorming, divergence |
| Paper prototypes | hand-drawn screens, cut-outs | hours | early flow & nav |
| Wireframes | Balsamiq, Figma greyscale | hours | structure, information architecture |
| Interactive wireframes | Figma click-through | day | flow + light interaction |
| Hi-fi mockups | Figma full-colour | days | visual design, brand |
| Hi-fi clickable | Figma + Smart Animate | days | usability tests, stakeholder demos |
| Coded prototype | minimal app/site | weeks | technical feasibility, performance |

### Low fidelity prototyping

Rough hand-drawn sketches (pencil & paper, post-it notes). Goal: **quick, cheap, easy to change**. Detail key elements and how the user interacts with them. Used to create multiple alternatives, test/evaluate ideas with users (e.g. Wizard of Oz), and get feedback on **interaction & concepts** rather than visuals.

- Cheap to throw away — encourages exploration of many ideas.
- Critique focuses on structure, not aesthetics.
- Anyone can contribute (non-designers).
- Less convincing for stakeholders/customers; limited evaluation of detailed interaction (animation, latency, exact wording).

### High fidelity prototyping

Interactive, often implemented (code or Adobe XD-like tools). Goal: realistic visuals & interaction for evaluation/refinement; some content real, some simulated. Used to test/evaluate with users (interaction/flow/usability) and to support investment/sales/marketing.

- Realistic feel — better usability testing for visual & micro-interactions; stakeholder buy-in; ready to hand to developers.
- Expensive to change — **anchoring effect**, people commit to embodied ideas; risk of users thinking it's a real product (e.g. unsafe to enter real data).

**Rule of thumb:** match fidelity to the *question you're testing*. Testing nav layout? Wireframes suffice. Testing a complex animated transition? Go hi-fi.

### Throw-away vs evolutionary

- **Throw-away:** prototype is a learning artefact, not future code — avoids "code rot".
- **Evolutionary:** prototype evolves into the production system — faster, but risks accumulating shortcuts.

The Cambridge syllabus emphasises **throw-away** prototyping early — separating "exploring the design space" from "engineering the system".

### Wizard of Oz

A human secretly drives behaviour the system would later automate. Particularly useful for prototyping ML/AI features before the model exists. Tests the UX of the *experience*, not the underlying tech.

### Why prototype (cost framing)

Prototyping is **cheap thinking aloud in a tangible artefact**. Design-change cost grows roughly **10×** per subsequent phase (requirements → design → implementation → test → production). Prototyping lets you explore and iterate before investment locks you in.

## 6 · Cognitive aspects for design

Three considerations for HCI design: **perception & cognition** (memory, visual perception, attention), **interaction** (affordances, form factor, input modalities, gestures), **content understanding** (information architecture, data modelling). Practical tools that generalise these: interaction design patterns, common widgets, UI frameworks, design systems.

### Memory — Atkinson–Shiffrin model (1968)

Three stores connected by cognitive processes; **rehearsal** loops within short-term memory; **retrieval** pulls from long-term back to short-term.

**Short-term memory (STM):** holds (but doesn't manipulate) a small amount of information in an active, readily available state, for a short time.
- **Duration** ≈ 15–30 seconds, extendable by rehearsal.
- **Capacity** ≈ 7 items, varies by type (≈7 digits, ≈6 letters, ≈5 words) — Miller's *"The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two"* (1956).
- **Chunking** extends capacity by grouping items, e.g. `7562986224741` vs `756 298 622 741`.

**Design implication — tooltips:** reduce STM load by showing relevant data on hover instead of forcing users to remember it across screens.

**Long-term memory (LTM):**
- **Explicit/declarative** — episodic (events/experiences, e.g. entering the building this morning), semantic (facts/concepts, e.g. capital of a country).
- **Implicit/procedural** — motor skills (tying shoelaces, riding a bike).

**Memorisation:** usually by repetition; also when something is surprising/unusual; we learn by **association** between pieces of information.

**Design implication — established conventions:** conventions reduce learning cost — users transfer existing memory (login/basket top-right, nav tabs along top, filters on left sidebar, e.g. Waterstones/Amazon). Same with code — similar syntax across Java/C#/Python lets a learner transfer a known mental model.

**Activation of content in memory** increases with: **practice** (how many times used), **recency** (how recently used), **context** (what else is in focus of attention).

**Recollection vs recognition (Yonelinas, 2002):**

| Property | Recognition | Recollection |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Faster | Slower |
| Sensitive to divided attention? | Less | More |
| Dependence | Independent of recollection | — |

**Design implication — recognition over recall:**
- **Labelling** — icons + text (e.g. Android clock app labels ALARM/CLOCK/TIMER/STOPWATCH instead of icons alone).
- **Similar functionality** — reuse visual patterns across products (heart icon for "love it", share-icon row).
- **Code completion** — IDEs surface options so users recognise rather than recall API names.

### Visual perception — Gestalt principles

The whole is perceived as more than the sum of its parts. Seven principles:

1. **Similarity** — *make equal elements look equal.* Group similar elements (shape, size, colour) into one perceptual unit. E.g. Google search results style each role consistently (title/URL/snippet); BBC News styles headlines/content/date/section distinctly. **Anomaly** (breaking similarity in shape/orientation) draws attention — used to show selection.
2. **Proximity** — *group related things.* Elements close in space are perceived as one unit (tight horizontal spacing reads as rows; tight vertical spacing reads as columns). E.g. Amazon footer link groups, Craigslist category columns.
3. **Enclosure** — *border related things.* Elements enclosed by a visual border belong together — drawn lines, background colours, or grey separator lines (BBC News story dividers, eBay product cards).
4. **Common fate** — *move related things together.* Elements that move together are perceived as belonging together (Amazon expanding menus, Netflix carousels sliding as a unit).
5. **Figure/ground** — *make content stand out from background.* We separate foreground (figure) from background (ground) — e.g. Gmail compose dialog over dimmed inbox, modal overlays, hero images with overlaid CTAs. **Figure-ground ambiguity** (Rubin's vase) is exploited in poster design.
6. **Closure** — *viewers fill in visual gaps.* Icons built from disconnected strokes still read as coffee cup, map pin, printer, wifi, phone; simple logos rely on this. ⚠ **Bias warning** — if the missing part isn't a simple shape, closure can mislead (e.g. a gap in a line chart is interpolated into a smooth curve even if the real data was jagged).
7. **Good continuation** — lines/curves are perceived as continuous even where they intersect other objects. Multi-series line charts: crossing lines are still tracked as continuous (helped by colour coding); decorative connecting paths guide the eye through a story.

### Attention

> *Selectively concentrating on a discrete aspect of information whilst ignoring other perceivable information.* Multi-modal (visual, auditory, verbal, tactile); incomplete, inaccurate, and limited.

**Types (Spotlight Model):** **reflexive/exogenous** (triggered by external cues) vs **intentional/endogenous** (triggered by internal cues). Small **focus**, surrounded by blurry **fringe**, beyond which is the **margin**.

**Reflexive vs intentional (Jonides, 1981):** external cues get more processing time and accuracy, are harder to ignore, remain effective under cognitive load, and draw attention even when unexpected.

**Design implication — control visual noise:** bad examples (`arngren.net`, `rudgwicksteamshow.co.uk`) overwhelm the visual system; cluttered code editors hide a missing brace among noise. Good choices: **notifications** (surface what matters, when it matters); **progressive disclosure** (reveal options as needed, e.g. Duolingo asks beginner-status, then runs a placement test, then drills exercises).

### Affordances

> Visual design that suggests and encourages the right interaction.

- A door handle's shape suggests wrapping a hand around it and rotating.
- A raised rectangle with a label suggests it can be **pressed** (button).
- A row of items cut off at the edge suggests **scrolling**.
- Underlined/coloured/arrow-marked text suggests **clicking**/external linking.

⚠ Affordances make UIs **easy to learn** — they are not innate; users still rely on prior experience of conventions.

### Physical form of user interfaces

What the user can do, and how, depends on UI type: **physical** (consumer electronics/appliances), **screen-based** (mobile/laptop/desktop — course focus), **wearables**, **speech**, **VR**, **tangible**, **robot**.

**Screen-based interfaces — two components:** **presentation device** (primary screen, external monitors) and **input device(s)** (touchscreen, touchpad, mouse, keyboard — physical or on-screen).

**Constraints on design:**
- **Form factor** — hardware dimensions: size (phone/tablet/laptop/multi-monitor), screen ratio (portrait/landscape, fixed/rotatable).
- **Input modality** — how the user interacts: input points (single-point/multi-touch), available gestures (point, click, double-click, scroll, hover, swipe, pinch, etc.).

**Examples:**
- *Form factor:* the same web app on phone/tablet/desktop has very different layouts — single column with hamburger menu on phone, multi-column persistent nav on desktop.
- *Input modality:* a map app on phone uses pinch-to-zoom (multi-touch); the same site on desktop requires ⌘+scroll (modifier + wheel).
- *Both combined:* video editing on laptop (Premiere Pro, mouse+keyboard, multi-panel UI) vs phone (CapCut, touch gestures, simplified timeline) — same task, fundamentally different IxD.

## 7 · Content, information architecture, and data modelling

### Information architecture (IA)

> The practice of deciding how to arrange the parts of something to be understandable.

**Aims:** organise content/functionality into **taxonomies and hierarchies**; define **relationships** between content/functionality; **tag** content with metadata; design **search optimisation** strategies.

**Process for developing an IA:**
1. **Inventory** — what content do you have, what else do you need?
2. **Establish a taxonomy** — high-level structure.
3. **Chunk** — divide into logical units.
4. **Diagram** — show structure and navigation between units.
5. **Analyse** — test with real users and revise.

Applies across the abstract/detail spectrum — whole system → single page → section.

### Taxonomy types

| Type | Relationship | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Flat** | Enumeration | List of items |
| **Hierarchy** | *is-a* (content ↔ category) | Sitemap tree |
| **Faceted** | *has-a* (content ↔ attributes) | Filterable product catalog |
| **Network** | Association (e.g. multiple tags) | Tag clouds |

Usually surfaced via **navigation** and **sitemaps**. **Card-sorting** elicits taxonomies from users' own perspective.

### IA diagramming best practices

- Focus on **structure**, not hierarchy — hierarchy can change, structure should not.
- **Processes should be logical** — page links should help the user achieve tasks.
- IA is part of **UCD** — based on user research.
- Consider the **user of the IA itself** (PM, dev team) — include what they need.
- IA must **change over time** as the system evolves.

### Page-level IA

- **Match user expectations** for content location — only move things if there's a rationale.
- **Consistency** and cognitive aspects help.
- **Wireframes** map out page structure — how elements are organised.

### Common mobile app page structures

- **Hierarchy/Catalog** — drill-down list (e.g. Sainsbury's).
- **Hub & Spoke** — central hub with detail pages (e.g. Google Maps place).
- **Nested Doll/Flow** — linear sequence (e.g. booking flow).
- **Tabbed View** — peer screens via tabs (e.g. Clock app).
- **Dashboard** — summary of key info (e.g. Fitbit).
- **Filtered View** — filtered list of items (e.g. Play Store top charts).

### Navigation

Moving between pages/screens to complete tasks. Three directions:
- **Lateral** — same level of hierarchy.
- **Forward** — top → down in hierarchy.
- **Reverse** — backwards, chronologically or hierarchically.

**Search** is an alternative navigation method for locating content quickly — often easier than navigating a complex hierarchy. IA must be complemented with UCD (requirements + testing).

### Data modelling

Frontend (UI) and backend (data structures, algorithms) both need design decisions. IA organises information *for the user*; data modelling asks how, and for what, the data/logic will be used. Trade-offs balance system performance *and* end-to-end user requirements.

**Example — article data structure:** a wireframe maps to a backing data structure:
```
article: { title, subtitle, date, author, hero-image,
           hero-image-credit, content, tags, comments, ... }
site:    { articles, recipes, ... }
```

**Example — tagging:** how tags will be used drives the model (add/remove tags on messages & conversations; organise tags in groups, possibly one tag → multiple groups; some tags need emphasis). Result: **no hierarchy** in the data model — tag objects are flat with metadata (`groups`, `filterable`, `type`, `visible`); messages/conversations only store the **tag id** (reference, not embed).

**Example — messaging SMS:**
- **Option A** — server stores conversations + users + messages explicitly: `User —1:n— Conversation —1:n— Message`.
- **Option B** — server only stores `Message` (from, to, text, date, status); the client reconstructs conversations & users. The backend needn't model abstractions the client can derive — it forwards message data; only the client has the *concept* of conversations & users.

**Data modelling principles:**
- Design with the user in mind.
- Keep structures flexible — systems evolve, features get added.
- Balance backend and frontend data needs — UI reshaping data can introduce bugs.
- Align system properties with user requirements — find what matters and where.
- Don't choose technical elegance over usability — the real world is messy.

## 8 · Practical tools for interaction design

Three overlapping tools — **interaction design patterns**, **UI toolkits**, **design systems** — each provides solutions/implementations of common IxD problems at different levels of detail.

### Interaction design patterns

**Origin:** Christopher Alexander's *A Pattern Language* (architecture) → GoF *Design Patterns* (software) → Tidwell's *Designing Interfaces* (UI).

**Why useful:** learning tool for UI design; quick solutions to common problems; ensure **consistency** and match **user expectations**; offer a **vocabulary** for discussing designs.

**Structure of a pattern (five components):** title, problem (what does it solve?), context (when to use it), solution (how to implement it), example.

| Pattern | Problem | Context | Solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Navigation Tabs** | User needs to navigate site/app sections with clear indication of current location | 2–10 sections; short, predictable, stable category titles; highest level of nav | Single-line horizontal row of tabs (usually top); whole tab area clickable, not just label; maintained across all pages |
| **Breadcrumbs** | User needs to navigate up/back in hierarchy and understand their location | Page is within a hierarchy (not top); other local nav doesn't expose hierarchy; user may arrive via external source (e.g. search) | Single-line list of labels from topmost page down to current page |
| **Carousel** | Browse a set of visually-representable similar items, possibly select one | Items represent well visually; limited screen space; 3–8 items shown at a time; possibly multiple carousels for categorised items | Horizontal row of items with thumbnails; nav controls (arrows) or scroll/swipe |

### UI toolkits

> A collection of UI components/widgets, usually styled with a common theme, used to build digital applications.

**Focus:** easy access to common interactive components + consistent theming.

**Perceived advantages:** easier to build by combining building blocks; better team communication (common design language); better usability (consistent appearance).

- **Bootstrap** — front-end open-source toolkit with pre-built components; for each component: demo, CSS class(es), example HTML, configuration options.
- **Untitled UI** — Figma UI kit + design system; components + variants, customisable styles (colour/typography/effects), example pages for common uses.

### Design systems

> A collection of reusable components, guided by clear standards, composable to build one or more applications.

Design systems = **interaction design patterns + UI kits + strict rules of usage.**

**Perceived advantages:** faster development via reusable components + shared rationale; unified language across cross-functional teams; improved usability (cohesive, consistent UX); improved maintenance and scalability.

**Example — Google Material Design:** design system for Android/iOS/Flutter/web.
- Guided by principles (e.g. *Material is the metaphor*, *Bold, graphic, intentional*, *Motion provides meaning*).
- Defines **components** as interactive building blocks with built-in states (focus, selection, activation, error, hover, press, drag, disabled).
- Supports **theming** — colour, typography, shape customisable per brand.
- Per-component docs cover **usage**, **anatomy** (labelled parts), **behaviour**, **placement**, **states**, **specs**.

### Summary and limitations

All three tools offer a shared vocabulary and solutions of varying detail. **Limitations:** a catalogue is **not a checklist** — every project has context, so pick the right pattern/component; some patterns solve the same problem differently; abstractions still leave appearance (size, colour, typography) and interactivity (screen size/ratio, touch vs click) to decide. **Not a replacement for UCD** — verify user needs first.

## 9 · User research data analysis

Data alone doesn't build empathy — it must be analysed to: relate what the data says about users; keep users in mind during design decisions; discuss implications with the team and users. Different data types need different analysis approaches.

### Quantitative vs qualitative

- **Quantitative** — numbers; analysis ascertains size, magnitude, amount, statistical significance.
- **Qualitative** — words, pictures, video; analysis identifies themes, patterns, stories. Can be turned into numbers (e.g. counts of themes) but at the cost of depth.

| Method | Qualitative example | Quantitative example |
|---|---|---|
| Interviews / Focus groups | Audio/video, interviewer notes, open-question responses | Demographics, closed questions |
| Observation | Observer notes, photos, think-aloud, behaviour records | Demographics, time on task, # people involved |
| Questionnaires | Open-question + "further comments" | Demographics, closed-question responses |
| Card sorting | Records of behaviour & comments | List of categories, items in each category |

### Quantitative analysis

- **Central tendency:** mean (sum ÷ count), median (middle ranked value), mode (most frequent value).
- **Variability:** range (highest − lowest), standard deviation $\sigma$ (amount of variation), variance ($\sigma^2$).
- **Categorical distribution** — percentages.
- **Graphical representations:** bar, pie, scatter, time series, histogram, maps. ⚠ Be careful not to mislead with numbers.

**Anscombe's Quartet:** four datasets with **identical** summary statistics ($\bar{x}, \bar{y}$, regression line, $R^2$) but very different shapes when plotted $\Rightarrow$ always **visualise** data — descriptive statistics alone can hide structure.

**Card sorting analysis:** build a pairwise **similarity (co-occurrence) matrix** — how often participants grouped each pair together. **Hierarchical clustering/dendrogram** visualises which items group at what dissimilarity level; cut at a threshold to extract clusters. Reveals participants' mental categories (e.g. tomatoes group with vegetables despite being botanically a fruit).

**Tools:** spreadsheets (simple stats/graphs); statistical packages (SPSS, STATA); visualisation (RAWGraphs, Tableau, Google Data Studio, Power BI); card-sorting (UXtweak, OptimalSort, UX Metrics).

### Qualitative analysis

**Basic approaches:** critical incidents (identify key events, apply specific techniques); identifying themes (emergent — **inductive**, may depend on observation framework); categorising data (pre-specified scheme — **deductive**). In practice, combine all three.

**Analytical frameworks:**

| Framework | Data | Focus | Expected outcomes | Granularity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Conversation analysis** | Recordings of spoken conversation | How conversations are conducted | Insights into how conversations are managed/progress | Word-level, pauses, inflection |
| **Discourse analysis** | Recordings of speech/writing | How words convey meaning | Implicit/hidden meaning in texts | Word, phrase, sentence |
| **Content analysis** | Any "text" (written, A/V, photos) | Frequency of items in text | Frequency of items appearing | Words → feelings → artefacts |
| **Interaction analysis** | Video of natural activity | Verbal/non-verbal interaction between people and artefacts | Insights about how knowledge/action are used within an activity | Artefact, dialogue, gesture |
| **Grounded theory** | Empirical data of any kind | Building a theory around the phenomenon | Theory grounded in empirical data | Varies |
| **Systems-based** | Large-scale, heterogeneous | Large-scale people + tech orgs (hospital, airport) | Insights about organisational effectiveness/efficiency | Macro/organisational |

**Thematic Analysis (TA):** codes (small labels for data fragments) → grouped into **themes** → optionally **sub-codes** under codes. Themes are emergent groupings of related codes (e.g. *Robot capabilities* covering *engagement*, *empathy*, *feedback*, *instruction*). Output usually paired with **representative quotes** to evidence each theme.

**Tools:** paper & markers, spreadsheets; NVivo, Atlas.ti, CAQDAS Networking Project, Coda.

### Presenting findings

Depends on **audience**, **purpose**, and **methods used**. Use graphical representations where helpful. Present an **evidence chain** showing how findings derive from underlying data (adds richness, guards against over-interpretation). Quant + qual are often combined (e.g. embodiment-preference scores alongside participant quotes).

## 10 · Keeping the user in mind: stakeholders, personas, scenarios, journey maps

Tools to summarise findings concretely and guide design: stakeholder analysis, personas, scenarios, journey maps.

### Stakeholder analysis

Identifies stakeholders, tracks their interests, prioritises product development.

- **Target users** — primary stakeholder(s); can span more than one group (e.g. CamCORS: students, supervisors, DoSes).
- **Other stakeholders** — those who **influence** the target user's interaction (e.g. parents managing a child's device use), are **affected** by it (e.g. friends of a runner using a trail map), or **support** it (e.g. helpdesk staff).

**Types — from the product (Eason, 1987):** **primary** (frequent, hands-on users), **secondary** (produce input/receive output but don't directly use it), **tertiary** (affected by introduction, or influence purchase), **facilitating** (involved in design/development/maintenance).

**Types — from the organisation:** **internal** (employees, managers, owners), **external** (customers/users, community/society, suppliers, shareholders, government).

**Stakeholder mapping (2×2 grid — Priority × Involvement):**

| | Low involvement | High involvement |
|---|---|---|
| **High priority** | Address their concerns | Involve them extensively |
| **Low priority** | Keep them informed | Involve them as needed |

**Challenges:** finding the right people; balancing different needs/priorities; engaging stakeholders at the right time.

### Personas

User archetypes constructed from research data — **fictitious, specific, concrete** representations of target users (not a real person, but a detailed example, e.g. "Betty"). Puts a face on the target user group as a design target ("would this work for Betty?") and helps make user-aligned decisions and trade-offs ("works for Betty but not Jake — try this instead").

**Types:**
- **Focal** — primary users; design optimised for them; ≥1 required, ≤3 recommended.
- **Secondary** — also use the product; satisfy when possible.
- **Unimportant** — low-priority (infrequent, unauthorised, unskilled, misusers).
- **Affected** — don't use the product but are affected (e.g. spouse of a travel-site user).
- **Exclusionary** — explicitly *not* designed for; stops non-users creeping back into discussions.

**Creating a persona:** (1) review user research on the stakeholder group, (2) identify common attributes, (3) identify differences from other groups, (4) describe a unified persona combining those attributes. Can be a **collage** of multiple users — the goal is empathy, not realism.

**What to include:** name, photo; demographics (gender, age, location, marital status, family); personality (a quote/slogan); product-specific — goals/needs/attitudes/motivations (not tasks), knowledge/proficiency, frustrations ("pain points"), context of usage.

**Challenges:** superficial/stereotypical representations; "what you see is all there is" bias (assuming the persona captures the whole user); cost of producing well-grounded personas. Mitigation: ground in real data, validate with users, and update as understanding evolves.

### Scenarios

> *"A concise description of a persona using a product to achieve a goal."* (Cooper, 2004)

- **Concise** — short but complete; breadth over depth.
- **Product** — software/device; assumed to exist.
- **Goal** — reason for performing the task.

**Why use them:** validate design and check assumptions; bridge from theoretical/conceptual design to lo-fi/wireframe; create shared understanding of users across the team (designers, marketers, engineers, execs).

**Creation:** developed in brainstorming sessions (e.g. scenario mapping workshops). Answers *what* the user wants, *why*, *how* they'll achieve it, and *what they're thinking*. Distinct from storyboards and user stories, but can underlie both.

**Types:**
- **Goal/task-based** — goal and reason only, no *how*; useful in design and usability testing.
- **Elaborated** — adds context and user details; holistic; mainly design stage.
- **Full-scale task** — includes the steps to accomplish the task; output of design stage, used by dev team or in usability testing to validate steps match real usage.

**Challenges:** too superficial; wrong level of detail; **linearity** (limited handling of unforeseen events).

### Journey maps

Visualisation of the process a person goes through to achieve a goal. Built up: persona + scenario + goals/actions ⟹ **timeline**; timeline + thoughts/emotions ⟹ **narrative**; narrative + opportunities/pain points ⟹ **journey map**. Useful for verifying the user's story makes sense and discussing product fit with stakeholders.

**Typical structure:** **phases** (e.g. Awareness → Adoption → Engagement → Retention → Contribution); **activities** at each phase; **emotions** (positive and negative, with representative quotes); **pain points** at each phase; optional opportunities/internal ownership/metrics.

**Challenges:** superficial if not based on real research; linearity; "what you see is all there is" bias; cost.

> ⚠ **Always remember:** people are diverse — don't assume all users will use the product the same way. Different devices, interaction techniques, assistive tech, and adaptive strategies bring different experiences, expectations, and preferences.

## 11 · Evaluation without users: heuristic evaluation

### Why evaluate?

- **Compare multiple designs** against user requirements; feed into a better one.
- **Verify** the solution meets requirements; if not, evaluation suggests fixes.
- Improve design as part of **iterative development**.

**Two categories** (course focuses on practical/quick methods = **"discount usability"**): **without users** (Heuristic Evaluation, Cognitive Walkthrough) vs **with users** (Usability Testing, Controlled Experiments).

### What heuristic evaluation is

> A method using a fixed set of good-practice **heuristics** to find common usability problems.

A **discount usability** technique — cheap, fast, no real users needed. Can evaluate sketches, prototypes, or production systems. **3–5 evaluators** each check the interface against the heuristics independently; compiled problems inform redesign.

### Process

1. **Pre-evaluation training** — heuristics & scenarios.
2. **Evaluate individually** — go through **twice** (overview, then detail).
3. **Collate results.**
4. **Rate severity** — individually, then discuss and agree.
5. **Feedback into design.**

### Severity ratings (Nielsen scale)

A combination of **frequency**, **persistence**, and **impact**.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | Not a usability problem |
| 1 | Cosmetic — fix if time permits |
| 2 | Minor — low priority |
| 3 | Major — important to fix |
| 4 | Catastrophe — imperative to fix |

### Nielsen's 10 heuristics in detail

1. **Visibility of system status** — keep users informed via appropriate, timely feedback. UI response thresholds: **\<200 ms** no indicators needed; **1 s** user notices delay; **10 s** max for user to stay focused on one action $\Rightarrow$ use progress bars/indicators.
2. **Match between system and the real world** — speak the user's language: familiar words, phrases, concepts, not internal jargon.
3. **User control and freedom** — users perform actions by mistake; need a clearly marked "emergency exit". **Total exit** → "home" button; **soft exit** → "back" button/Esc.
4. **Consistency and standards** — different words/situations/actions shouldn't mean the same thing; follow platform conventions (e.g. burger menu top-left, search top-right).
5. **Error prevention** — good error messages matter, but the best designs **prevent problems** outright (autocomplete, caps-lock warning).
6. **Recognition rather than recall** — make elements/actions/options **visible**; minimise memory load. Menus, breadcrumbs, labelled icons promote recognition.
7. **Flexibility and efficiency of use** — shortcuts (hidden from novices) speed up expert use; cater to both, and let users tailor frequent actions.
8. **Aesthetic and minimalist design** — no irrelevant information; every extra unit competes with relevant ones.
9. **Help users recognise, diagnose, and recover from errors** — plain language (no error codes), precisely indicate the problem, suggest a constructive solution.
10. **Help and documentation** — best if the design needs no explanation, but docs may still be necessary. **Proactive help** (e.g. templates offered upfront) vs **reactive help** (e.g. help menu always available).

### Analysis: cost-effectiveness

- **Discount method** — benefit-cost ratio of **48** (Nielsen, 1994), e.g. $10,500 cost for $500,000 benefit. Value: in-house ⇒ productivity; open market ⇒ sales.
- **1 evaluator** finds **~35%** of problems.
- **5 evaluators** find **~75%** of problems.
- **Diminishing returns** — more evaluators cost more but find few additional problems; benefit/cost peaks at **4–5 evaluators**.

**Pros:** cheap & quick; easy to learn; finds many problems.
**Cons:** not task-focused; no actual users; not rigorous; can be too generic for new products.

### Derivations

Nielsen's 10 is one set — designers may make their own, e.g. **Andy Budd's *Heuristics for Modern Web Application Development*** (2007): user expectations, clarity, minimising complexity/cognitive load, efficiency, context, consistency/standards, error prevention, error recovery, enjoyable UX.

### Heuristic eval vs user testing

| Aspect | Heuristic eval | User testing |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Low | Medium–high |
| Speed | Days | Weeks |
| Real users | No | Yes |
| Catches catastrophes | Yes | Yes |
| Catches subtle real-world issues | Poor | Good |
| Captures emotion/context | No | Yes |
| Captures cognitive load | Weak | Good |

**Use both** — heuristic eval first to clean up obvious violations, then user testing to uncover what experts miss.

## 12 · Cognitive walkthrough

> A **task-centred** evaluation by experts to find usability problems for **first-time users**.

Originates from **CE+ theory** (Polson, Lewis, 1990) — models the user as someone who tries actions and learns from feedback. Focuses on real, complete, representative tasks; evaluates problems first-time users hit without training; imagines behaviour of **entire classes of users** (cf. personas), not one. Most effective when evaluators can picture the real environment of use.

**Useful for finding:** assumptions about user thinking; missing/hard-to-find controls; inadequate feedback; difficulties with labels/prompts.

### Process

1. **Define inputs:**
   - **Users** — target group; record knowledge of similar systems.
   - **Sample tasks** — representative (most frequent/important); described from the user's goal-oriented perspective (e.g. "send a £20 transfer to a friend", not "explore the app").
   - **Action sequences** — correct steps to complete each task; granularity depends on task/product/user expertise.
   - **Interface description** — storyboard, interactive prototype, or implemented product.
2. **Get the analysts** — usually usability experts (not users, unlike HE); team members can participate but must "unlearn" the design. Training needed: CW method plus cognitive science & interaction design.
3. **Walk through action sequences** — tell a **credible story** for each action, asking the four questions below.
4. **Record:** user knowledge requirements; assumptions about user population (likely to know it, why?); side issues & design changes; the credible success or failure story.
5. **Revise the interface** — user fails to select right action → eliminate it, prompt user, or signal availability; user doesn't know correct action is available → make it more obvious; user doesn't know which action is correct → label controls based on user knowledge, check sequence makes sense; user can't tell things are going OK → give feedback. Many problems are system-user mental-model mismatches → **global** solutions, not local fixes.

### The four questions (per step)

1. **Will the user try to achieve the right effect?** — do they have the *intent* this step expects?
2. **Will the user notice that the correct action is available?** — is the control visible and identifiable?
3. **Will the user associate the correct action with the effect they want?** — does the label/icon match their mental model?
4. **If the correct action is performed, will the user see that progress is being made toward the goal?** — is the feedback clear?

A "no" to any question = a usability defect — record the step, which question failed, why, and severity/suggested fix.

### Success vs failure stories

- **Credible success story** — all success criteria met.
- **Failure story** — at least one criterion not met.

### Analysis

**Strengths:** finds **severe problems** fairly well (comparable to other techniques); finds **specific** rather than general problems; strong on **learnability** (focus on first-time users).

**Limitations:** cannot evaluate every task users will perform; users may take different action sequences; each task is evaluated **separately**, so cross-task interactions are missed. Pair with a task-free user-centred method (e.g. HE) to catch what CW misses.

### When to use cognitive walkthrough

- **Before** the system is fielded.
- **Onboarding-critical** systems — kiosks, ATMs, public services where users get one shot.
- Where you don't have access to real users.
- Cheaper than user testing for *learnability*.

### Streamlined cognitive walkthrough (Spencer, 2000)

- Drop questions 1 and 4 in early walkthroughs (too speculative).
- Focus on questions 2 and 3 — visibility and labelling — the most common UI failures.
- Run as a group with explicit "ground rules" (no design defence, no premature solutions).

### Cognitive walkthrough vs heuristic evaluation

| Aspect | CW | Heuristic eval |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Learnability for *new* users | Broad usability |
| Granularity | Per-step, per-task | Per-screen, per-element |
| Output | Step-level defect list | Violation list with severity |
| Best for | Onboarding, infrequent-use systems | Mature UI polish |
| Skill required | Task analysis + cognitive theory | Usability heuristics |

Many practitioners run both — CW first to validate task flow, then heuristic eval to polish details.

### Derivations

Applies to **APIs** — evaluates whether participants can build an accurate mental model of the API from code alone (O'Callaghan, 2010, *The API Walkthrough Method*).

## 13 · Evaluation with users: usability testing and controlled experiments

| Type | Data | Cost | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Usability testing** | Qualitative (behaviour, speech) | Cheaper | Find problems, feed into design |
| **Controlled experiments** | Quantitative (speed, accuracy, error rate) | More expensive | Compare 2+ designs when metrics matter |

### What usability testing is

> Testing a product with representative users to collect feedback for improving the design.

ISO 9241-11 defines usability as the **effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction** with which specified users achieve specified goals in a specified context of use.

**Uses:** gain info from the user's perspective (is it usable? which features work? what problems?); find out satisfaction (would they use it? what can improve?); make **design recommendations**; establish **benchmarks** for future comparisons.

### Formative vs summative

- **Formative** — *during* development. Goal: find problems to fix. Iterative.
- **Summative** — *after* development (or for benchmarking). Goal: measure how good it is. Larger samples, statistical comparisons.

### Designing the study (8 steps)

1. **Decide objectives** — clear, testable; motivates tasks, data collection, interview questions.
2. **Participants** — match the target user; define inclusion criteria.
3. **Prepare task instructions** — realistic, scenario-based, non-leading ("find the cheapest flight from London to Tokyo on the 10th", not "click the search button").
4. **Find a location/environment** — lab vs remote vs field; control vs ecological validity.
5. **Pilot** — run 1–2 trial sessions; refine tasks/protocol.
6. **Recruit** — Nielsen rule of thumb: 5 users find ~85% of issues *in one iteration*, but multiple iterations and diverse subgroups need more.
7. **Conduct test** — greeting & intro → informed consent → background questionnaire (optional) → task instructions → task performance & recording (facilitator + observer + screen/audio/face recording) → debrief (optionally with questionnaire/interview).
8. **Analyse and report findings** — code observations, identify patterns, rate severity.

### Ethical considerations

An ethical usability test should have: **minimal risk**; **informed consent**; **minimise deception** (generally not needed); participant may **withdraw at any time**; data kept **confidential**; participant may **withdraw their data**.

**Informed consent must state:** purpose of study; what the participant will do; right to withdraw at any time without reason; how results will be used and kept confidential; right to have their results excluded.

Other practical considerations: **anonymisation** of recordings; usability sessions can feel like exams — reassure participants ("we're testing the system, not you"); fair **compensation** for time and travel.

### Think-aloud protocol

Participants speak whatever comes to mind as they do tasks — e.g. *"I'm looking for a button to save… I don't see it… maybe under 'File'?"*. Reveals their mental model, identifies the **locus of attention**, and spots frustration/confusion.

- **Concurrent** — speak as they go. Risk: interferes with task performance; cognitively demanding tasks may cause participants to stop talking or be distracted.
- **Retrospective** — replay video, narrate after the fact. Captures performance better but relies on recall.

### Common metrics

- **Success rate** — % of users completing the task.
- **Time on task** — median preferred (mean is sensitive to outliers).
- **Errors** — count per task, severity-weighted.
- **Efficiency** — pathway length vs optimal.
- **Subjective** — Likert satisfaction; preference between alternatives.
- **System Usability Scale (SUS)** — 10 statements alternating positive/negative on a 5-point Likert scale; computed score 0–100. SUS > 68 is "above average". Quick, robust, comparable across systems.

### Variants

- **Cafe studies (guerilla usability testing)** — short tests in a public place for quick, cheap feedback. Good for walk-up-and-use apps, first use, onboarding/learning curve. Challenges: recruiting, noisy/uncomfortable locations.
- **Wizard-of-Oz** — participant interacts with a prototype whose behaviour is enacted by a human, believing it's the computer. Used for systems that don't yet exist, or when reaction to "the real thing" matters (usually for ML systems). Challenge: too realistic → users think it's ready to use.

### How many participants?

- **~5 users** gives best returns (time vs problems found) — same diminishing returns as HE/CW.
- Better to **iterate**: test → fix → test again (new problems may appear).
- Multiple target user groups → test with **3–5 per group**.

### Dogfooding

> Asking your **own employees** to use your own product.

**Used when prospective-user studies are hard:** commercial confidentiality; need a critical mass of users/usage time; hard to incentivise early users or get honest, detailed feedback.

**Drawbacks:** employees aren't representative of target users; bias toward internal norms; reluctance to give negative feedback to colleagues.

### Remote vs in-person

- **In-person** — richer non-verbal signal; lab environment unrealistic; easier task moderation.
- **Remote moderated** — diverse participants; participant uses own setup (ecological validity); harder to read body language.
- **Remote unmoderated** (UserTesting.com style) — scale, lower cost; less depth; sample bias toward platform users.

### Choosing an evaluation method

Depends on: time, cost, detail, and risk of the method; type of understanding required; context (consumer/enterprise/service); user activities (sequential vs overlapping subtasks, complex vs simple information, layman vs skilled practitioner). Mirrors the user research method choices — evaluation and research methods inform one another.

## 14 · Accessibility and inclusive design

### Why design accessibly?

- **Legal:** Equality Act 2010 (UK), ADA (US), EU Web Accessibility Directive — public-sector sites must meet WCAG 2.1 AA.
- **Ethical:** ~15% of the population has a disability per WHO, and many more have temporary or situational impairments.
- **Practical:** accessibility benefits everyone — captions for crowded environments, keyboard nav for power users, plain language for non-native speakers.

### WCAG principles — POUR

1. **Perceivable** — info presented so all users can perceive it (alt text, captions, contrast).
2. **Operable** — UI usable by all (keyboard, time limits, no seizure-inducing flashes).
3. **Understandable** — text and behaviour predictable.
4. **Robust** — works with assistive tech (semantic HTML, ARIA used correctly).

**Conformance levels:** **A** (essential), **AA** (legal baseline often), **AAA** (gold standard, sometimes impractical).

### Keyboard accessibility

- Every interactive element reachable by **Tab**; logical tab order matches visual order.
- Visible focus indicator (`:focus-visible` outline).
- Skip-to-content link at top of pages.
- No "keyboard traps" — can always escape.

### Screen readers

- Semantic HTML elements (`<button>`, `<nav>`, `<h1–6>`, `<main>`) carry meaning the screen reader announces.
- Use **ARIA only where HTML can't express the role**; first rule of ARIA: don't use ARIA if a native element exists.
- Provide **alt text** for non-decorative images; empty `alt=""` for decorative ones.
- **Live regions** (`aria-live`) for dynamic updates.
- Test with VoiceOver (macOS), NVDA (Windows), JAWS (Windows), TalkBack (Android).

### Visual accessibility

- **Contrast:** WCAG AA ≥ 4.5:1 for body text, ≥ 3:1 for large text.
- **Colour-blindness:** don't convey info by colour alone — pair with shape/text.
- **Resizable text** to 200% without breaking layout.
- **Avoid auto-playing motion** that distracts vestibular-sensitive users (`prefers-reduced-motion`).

### Cognitive accessibility

- Plain language — short sentences, common words.
- Consistent navigation; predictable behaviour.
- Avoid time pressure where avoidable; allow extension.
- Provide multiple representations (text + diagram + video).

### Inclusive design vs accessibility

- **Accessibility** focuses on disability.
- **Inclusive design** is broader — design for the widest reasonable range of human variability, including disability, age, language, culture, expertise, device, environment, network. Microsoft's *Inclusive Design Toolkit*: "Solve for one, extend to many."
- **Curb-cut effect** — designing for the edge often improves the centre (curb cuts for wheelchairs also help parents with strollers, delivery couriers, travellers with luggage).

### Universal design — 7 principles (Mace, 1997)

1. Equitable use.
2. Flexibility in use.
3. Simple and intuitive.
4. Perceptible information.
5. Tolerance for error.
6. Low physical effort.
7. Size and space for approach and use.

## Common pitfalls

- **"User likes it" ≠ "user can use it"** — preference and performance dissociate; don't substitute satisfaction surveys for task-based testing.
- **Recruiting friends/colleagues** for research or testing biases findings toward people who think like the design team.
- **Heuristic evaluation finds violations but not necessarily impact** — pair with usability testing to confirm real-world severity.
- **Skipping low-fi prototyping** "to save time" causes costly late-stage changes once fundamental flaws surface (10× cost-escalation rule).
- **Testing too late** — "design it then test it" is far costlier than building evaluation into the iterative cycle from the start.
- **Hi-fi prototypes mistaken for finished products** by stakeholders ("it looks done, ship it") and by users (entering real data into a non-functional prototype).
- **A single heuristic evaluator** finds only ~35% of problems — aim for 3–5 evaluators (diminishing returns beyond ~5).
- **Cognitive walkthrough done by the system's own designer** — cognitive bias; "unlearning" the design is essential for credible analysts.
- **Closure (Gestalt) can mislead** — a chart with a gap is perceptually "completed" into a smooth curve even when real data was jagged; don't rely on viewers spotting missing data.
- **ARIA misuse** — slapping ARIA roles onto inaccessible JS widgets usually breaks more than it fixes; prefer semantic HTML.
- **Confusing "what users would do" with "what the team thinks is sensible"** — both CW and HE require modelling the *user's* mental model, not the designer's.
- **Personas/journey maps treated as "the whole user"** — the "what you see is all there is" bias; people are diverse (devices, assistive tech, expectations).

## Exam strategy

- **For "evaluate this design"** — pick **3 Nielsen heuristics**, cite specific UI elements that violate them, and give a severity rating (0–4) with justification (frequency × persistence × impact).
- **For "design a study"** — follow the structure: research question → participants → tasks → environment → pilot → recruit → run → analyse, plus ethics (informed consent, withdrawal, confidentiality).
- **Quote the UCD cycle** verbatim if asked: understand context → specify requirements → produce design solutions → evaluate against requirements → iterate.
- **Quote the cognitive walkthrough's four questions verbatim** and apply them per-step for an onboarding-flow question, rather than dumping heuristics.
- **For requirements questions**, use the Wiegers & Beatty hierarchy (business → user → functional requirements) and classify functional vs non-functional/quality requirements explicitly.
- **For "why is X usable/unusable"**, reach for Gestalt principles (similarity, proximity, enclosure, common fate, figure/ground, closure, good continuation) and memory/attention implications (chunking, recognition > recall, progressive disclosure).
- **For accessibility questions**, quote **POUR** and pair each issue with a concrete impairment and WCAG level; cite the curb-cut effect for "why inclusive design helps everyone".
- **For prototyping fidelity questions**, justify by *what you intend to evaluate* — state the fidelity, the artefact, the participants, and the metric you'll record.
